## Explanation

### Why Functional Form Matters

OLS assumes the relationship between $y$ and $x$ is **linear in parameters** — but it does not require linearity in the original variables. By transforming variables before estimating, we can capture a wide range of non-linear economic relationships while keeping the machinery of OLS intact.

Logarithmic transformations are by far the most common such device in applied econometrics.

> **Convention**: In econometrics, $\log$ always means the **natural logarithm** $\ln$ (base $e \approx 2.718$). It never means $\log_{10}$. Software commands like `log()` in Stata and R also default to the natural log.

---

### The Four Functional Forms

#### 1. Level-Level

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u$$

**Interpretation**: A one-unit increase in $x$ is associated with a $\beta_1$-unit change in $y$.

$$\Delta y = \beta_1 \,\Delta x$$

Use when both variables are naturally measured in levels and the relationship is approximately linear (e.g. test score on class size).

---

#### 2. Log-Level (Semi-Elasticity)

$$\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u$$

**Interpretation**: A one-unit increase in $x$ is associated with an approximate $100\beta_1$ **percent** change in $y$.

$$\% \Delta y \approx 100 \beta_1 \,\Delta x$$

This is called a **semi-elasticity** — $x$ is in levels, $y$ is in logs. Use when $y$ is right-skewed (wages, income, prices) and the effect of $x$ is proportional rather than additive.

*Example*: If $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.082$ in a log-wage regression on years of education, then each additional year of education is associated with approximately an 8.2% increase in wages.

---

#### 3. Level-Log

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(x) + u$$

**Interpretation**: A 1% increase in $x$ is associated with a $\beta_1/100$ unit change in $y$.

$$\Delta y \approx \frac{\beta_1}{100}\,\% \Delta x$$

Use when $x$ exhibits diminishing marginal impact — each extra unit of $x$ matters less than the previous (e.g. $y$ = test score, $x$ = expenditure per student).

---

#### 4. Log-Log (Constant Elasticity)

$$\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(x) + u$$

**Interpretation**: A 1% increase in $x$ is associated with a $\beta_1$% change in $y$. Here $\beta_1$ is the **elasticity** of $y$ with respect to $x$.

$$\% \Delta y \approx \beta_1 \,\% \Delta x$$

This is the workhorse model for demand equations and production functions. The elasticity is **constant** across all values of $x$.

*Example*: If $\hat{\beta}_1 = -0.5$ in a log-log demand equation, the price elasticity of demand is $-0.5$ (inelastic).

---

### Summary Table

| Model | Dep. Var. | Indep. Var. | Interpretation of $\hat{\beta}_1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level-level | $y$ | $x$ | $\Delta y = \hat{\beta}_1 \Delta x$ (units) |
| Log-level | $\log(y)$ | $x$ | $\% \Delta y \approx 100\hat{\beta}_1 \Delta x$ (semi-elasticity) |
| Level-log | $y$ | $\log(x)$ | $\Delta y \approx (\hat{\beta}_1/100)\% \Delta x$ |
| Log-log | $\log(y)$ | $\log(x)$ | $\% \Delta y \approx \hat{\beta}_1 \% \Delta x$ (elasticity) |

---

### When to Use Logs

Use logarithmic transformations when:

1. **The variable is right-skewed** — wages, income, housing prices, firm revenues all tend to have long right tails. Taking logs compresses the distribution and makes OLS better behaved.
2. **You want an elasticity interpretation** — log-log gives a clean, scale-free elasticity.
3. **The marginal effect is proportional rather than additive** — if a $1 increase in $x$ has a larger absolute effect at low values of $y$ than at high values, a log-level model is more appropriate.
4. **To reduce heteroskedasticity** — log transformations often stabilise variance across observations.

---

### Limitations: What You Cannot Log

The natural log is undefined for zero and negative values:

$$\log(0) = -\infty, \quad \log(x) \text{ undefined for } x < 0$$

**Alternatives when $y \geq 0$ with zeros**:

- **$\log(1 + y)$**: Adds 1 before taking logs. Simple, but coefficient interpretation is non-standard; the approximation $\%\Delta y \approx 100\hat{\beta}_1\Delta x$ holds only when $y$ is large.
- **Inverse Hyperbolic Sine (IHS)**: $\text{arcsinh}(y) = \log(y + \sqrt{y^2 + 1})$. Behaves like $\log(2y)$ for large $y$, equals 0 at $y=0$, and is defined for negative values. Increasingly preferred in modern applied work.
- **Two-part (Tobit-type) models**: Separate models for the zero/non-zero decision and the level conditional on being positive.

## Worked Example

**Dataset**: Cross-sectional sample of $n = 526$ workers (Wooldridge `WAGE1`).

**Equation estimated**:

$$\log(\widehat{wage}_i) = 0.584 + 0.082\, educ_i + 0.029\, exper_i - 0.0006\, exper_i^2$$

**Coefficient on education**: $\hat{\beta}_{educ} = 0.082$

**Interpretation**: Each additional year of education is associated with approximately **8.2% higher wages**, holding experience constant.

This is a **log-level** model. The dependent variable is $\log(wage)$; education is measured in years (a level).

**Why log wages?** Wages are right-skewed: a few very high earners pull the distribution. Taking logs produces a more symmetric distribution and stabilises variance. The percentage interpretation is also more economically natural ("a 10% wage premium") than an additive one ("$0.82/hour more").

**Exact vs approximate interpretation**: The exact percentage effect of a one-unit change in $x$ in a log-level model is:

$$\% \Delta y = 100 \left(e^{\hat{\beta}_1} - 1\right)$$

For $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.082$: $100(e^{0.082} - 1) = 100(1.0854 - 1) = 8.54\%$. The approximation ($8.2\%$) is close enough for small coefficients but use the exact formula for coefficients above $\approx 0.2$.

---

## Common Misconception

> **"Log in a regression means log base 10."**

**Correction**: In econometrics, $\log$ universally refers to the **natural logarithm** (base $e$), not $\log_{10}$. When Wooldridge writes $\log(wage)$, he means $\ln(wage)$. Stata's `gen lwage = log(wage)` and R's `log(wage)` both compute natural logs. If you use $\log_{10}$ by accident, your coefficients will be off by a factor of $\ln(10) \approx 2.303$ and your interpretations will be wrong. Always double-check which base your software uses (hint: it's always $e$ by default in econometrics packages).

## Practice Prompts

**Prompt 1 — Identification**
A researcher estimates:

$$\widehat{price} = 12.3 + 0.95\,\log(sqft)$$

where $price$ is in thousands of dollars and $sqft$ is house size in square feet. What functional form is this? Interpret $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.95$. If house size increases by 10%, what is the predicted change in price (in dollars)?

---

**Prompt 2 — Model choice**
You are studying the effect of advertising expenditure on firm sales. Sales range from \$50,000 to \$50 million across firms. Advertising expenditure ranges from \$1,000 to \$500,000. Which functional form would you choose and why? What does your choice imply about the marginal return to advertising?

---

**Prompt 3 — Zero values**
A researcher wants to estimate the effect of government transfers on household savings. Savings takes the value zero for 35% of households in the sample. Why is $\log(savings)$ problematic here? Name two alternatives and briefly explain each.
