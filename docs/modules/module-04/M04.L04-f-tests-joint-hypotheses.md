## Explanation

### Why We Need Joint Tests

Suppose we want to know whether *education* and *experience* are **together** relevant predictors of wages. We might think: just check whether both have $|t| > 1.96$. But this is wrong.

If the t-tests for $\hat{\beta}_1$ and $\hat{\beta}_2$ are correlated (which they are whenever the regressors are correlated), then:
- Two individually insignificant variables can be **jointly significant**.
- Two individually significant variables can (in principle) be jointly redundant.

The correct tool for testing multiple restrictions simultaneously is the **F-test**.

---

### Setting Up the Hypotheses

A joint hypothesis imposes $q$ linear restrictions on the $\beta$ vector. The most common case is testing that $q$ specific coefficients are all zero:

$$H_0: \beta_1 = 0,\; \beta_2 = 0,\; \ldots,\; \beta_q = 0$$
$$H_1: \text{at least one of } \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_q \neq 0$$

For example, in a model with 5 regressors, we might test whether the last two are jointly irrelevant: $H_0: \beta_4 = 0, \beta_5 = 0$ ($q = 2$ restrictions).

---

### Restricted vs. Unrestricted Models

The F-test compares two regressions:

**Unrestricted model (UR):** includes all regressors (k total); has $SSR_{ur}$ and $R^2_{ur}$.

**Restricted model (R):** imposes $H_0$ by dropping the $q$ restricted regressors; has $SSR_r$ and $R^2_r$.

Imposing restrictions always **increases** SSR (or at best leaves it unchanged). The question is: does the increase in SSR from imposing $H_0$ represent more than we'd expect by chance?

---

### The F-Statistic

$$\boxed{F = \frac{(SSR_r - SSR_{ur})\,/\,q}{SSR_{ur}\,/\,(n-k-1)}}$$

where:
- $SSR_r - SSR_{ur}$ = increase in residual sum of squares from imposing restrictions
- $q$ = number of restrictions (numerator df)
- $n - k - 1$ = degrees of freedom of the unrestricted model (denominator df)

Under $H_0$ (and CLM assumptions MLR.1–MLR.6):

$$F \sim F_{q,\; n-k-1}$$

An F-distribution with $q$ and $n-k-1$ degrees of freedom. Since larger F means larger deterioration in fit under $H_0$, we always use a **right-tailed** test:

$$\text{Reject } H_0 \text{ at level } \alpha \iff F > F_{q,\; n-k-1,\; \alpha}$$

---

### Equivalent $R^2$ Form

Since $SSR = SST(1 - R^2)$ and $SST$ is the same in restricted and unrestricted models (when the restricted model still includes an intercept):

$$\boxed{F = \frac{(R^2_{ur} - R^2_r)\,/\,q}{(1 - R^2_{ur})\,/\,(n-k-1)}}$$

This form is convenient when software reports $R^2$ rather than $SSR$.

---

### Overall Significance of the Regression

A special case: the null hypothesis that **all** slope coefficients are zero (none of the $k$ regressors help explain $y$):

$$H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_k = 0 \qquad (q = k \text{ restrictions})$$

Under $H_0$, the restricted model is just $y_i = \beta_0 + u_i$, so $R^2_r = 0$, and the F-statistic simplifies to:

$$F = \frac{R^2_{ur}/k}{(1 - R^2_{ur})/(n-k-1)}$$

This is the **overall F-statistic** reported by regression software. Rejecting this $H_0$ means the regression has some explanatory power beyond just the mean of $y$.

---

### Why Individual t-Tests Are Not Sufficient for Joint Hypotheses

Suppose we test $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ and $H_0: \beta_2 = 0$ individually at 5% each. With two independent tests, the probability of making **at least one** Type I error (false rejection) is:

$$1 - (1 - 0.05)^2 = 1 - 0.9025 = 9.75\%$$

For correlated tests (which is typical in regression), the size distortion can be even worse or better depending on the correlation direction. The F-test controls the **joint** size at exactly $\alpha$.

Moreover, even if both t-statistics are individually below their critical values, the F-statistic can exceed its critical value if both estimators are simultaneously pushed in the same direction by the data.

---

## Worked Example

**Setup.** We estimate a log-wage regression with $n = 526$ workers and two potential regressors beyond education and gender: $\text{exper}$ and $\text{exper}^2$.

**Unrestricted model** ($k = 4$ regressors: educ, exper, exper², female):
$$\log(\hat{\text{wage}}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{educ} + \beta_2\,\text{exper} + \beta_3\,\text{exper}^2 + \beta_4\,\text{female} + u$$
$$SSR_{ur} = 97.34, \quad R^2_{ur} = 0.3625, \quad df_{ur} = 526 - 4 - 1 = 521$$

**Restricted model** ($k^* = 2$ regressors: educ, female — drops exper and exper²):
$$\log(\hat{\text{wage}}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{educ} + \beta_4\,\text{female} + v$$
$$SSR_r = 103.79, \quad R^2_r = 0.3201$$

**Joint hypothesis:** $H_0: \beta_2 = 0,\, \beta_3 = 0$ ($q = 2$ restrictions).

---

**Step 1 — State hypotheses:**
$$H_0: \beta_2 = 0 \text{ and } \beta_3 = 0 \qquad H_1: \text{at least one } \neq 0$$

**Step 2 — Compute F-statistic (SSR form):**
$$F = \frac{(SSR_r - SSR_{ur})\,/\,q}{SSR_{ur}\,/\,(n-k-1)} = \frac{(103.79 - 97.34)\,/\,2}{97.34\,/\,521}$$

$$= \frac{6.45\,/\,2}{97.34\,/\,521} = \frac{3.225}{0.1868} = 17.26$$

**Verification via R² form:**
$$F = \frac{(0.3625 - 0.3201)\,/\,2}{(1 - 0.3625)\,/\,521} = \frac{0.0424\,/\,2}{0.6375\,/\,521} = \frac{0.0212}{0.001224} = 17.32$$

(Small rounding difference; both $\approx 17.3$.)

**Step 3 — Distribution under $H_0$:** $F \sim F_{2,\, 521}$

**Step 4 — Critical value:** At $\alpha = 1\%$, $F_{2,\, 521, 0.01} \approx 4.62$. At $\alpha = 5\%$, $F_{2,\, 521, 0.05} \approx 3.00$.

**Step 5 — Decision:** $17.3 \gg 4.62$. **Strongly reject $H_0$** at any conventional significance level.

**Step 6 — Interpretation:** Experience and experience-squared are jointly highly significant. Despite experience and $\text{exper}^2$ being correlated (which can inflate individual SEs), together they explain substantial variation in log wages beyond education and gender.

---

### Connecting to Individual t-Tests

For context, individual t-statistics from the unrestricted model might be:
- $t_{\text{exper}} = 0.033 / 0.008 = 4.13$ (significant at 1%)
- $t_{\text{exper}^2} = -0.0005 / 0.0001 = -5.00$ (significant at 1%)

Here individual t-tests also indicate significance, consistent with the F-test. **But this won't always be the case:** see the misconception section below.

---

## Common Misconception

> **"Two individually insignificant t-tests mean the variables are jointly insignificant."**

Consider a model where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are highly positively correlated. The individual standard errors of $\hat{\beta}_1$ and $\hat{\beta}_2$ are inflated by multicollinearity. Individually, neither may pass the threshold $|t| > 1.96$. Yet the F-test may strongly reject $H_0: \beta_1 = 0, \beta_2 = 0$.

**Numerical illustration.** Suppose:

| | $\hat{\beta}_j$ | $se$ | $t$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x_1$ | 1.0 | 0.8 | 1.25 |
| $x_2$ | 0.9 | 0.8 | 1.13 |

Both t-statistics are below 1.96 — individually insignificant at 5%. Yet if $\hat{\beta}_1$ and $\hat{\beta}_2$ are negatively correlated (induced by positive correlation between $x_1$ and $x_2$), the F-statistic for their joint significance may exceed the critical value $F_{2, n-k-1, 0.05} \approx 3.0$.

**Conversely:** two individually significant coefficients could (in principle) be jointly just significant, though in practice this is less common.

**Rule:** Always use the F-test when testing joint hypotheses. Individual t-tests are correct only for single-coefficient tests.

## Practice Prompts

**Q1.** A model of house prices has $n = 148$ observations and $k = 5$ regressors. You want to test whether the three neighbourhood characteristic variables (school quality, crime rate, pollution) are jointly significant. The unrestricted model has $SSR_{ur} = 12{,}450$ and the restricted model (dropping those three variables) has $SSR_r = 14{,}200$. Compute the F-statistic, state its null distribution, find the 5% critical value, and give your conclusion.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1:** $H_0: \beta_{\text{school}} = \beta_{\text{crime}} = \beta_{\text{pollution}} = 0$; $q = 3$ restrictions.

**Step 2:**
$$F = \frac{(14{,}200 - 12{,}450)\,/\,3}{12{,}450\,/\,(148-5-1)} = \frac{1{,}750\,/\,3}{12{,}450\,/\,142} = \frac{583.33}{87.68} = 6.66$$

**Step 3:** Under $H_0$: $F \sim F_{3,\, 142}$.

**Step 4:** $F_{3,\, 142,\, 0.05} \approx 2.67$.

**Step 5:** $6.66 > 2.67$. **Reject $H_0$** at 5%. The neighbourhood variables are jointly significant.
</details>

---

**Q2.** In the same house price regression, the individual t-statistics are: $t_{\text{school}} = 1.45$, $t_{\text{crime}} = -1.50$, $t_{\text{pollution}} = 1.60$ (all with $df = 142$). The two-sided 5% critical value is $t_{142, 0.025} \approx 1.976$. All three fail individual significance tests. Is this contradictory with the F-test result in Q1? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No contradiction. This is the classic case where multicollinearity inflates individual standard errors, making each coefficient appear imprecise on its own, while the variables collectively provide substantial explanatory power.

The F-test accounts for the joint distribution of the estimators — including their covariances — and correctly identifies that the three variables together significantly improve fit. Individual t-tests ignore the correlations between $\hat{\beta}_{\text{school}}$, $\hat{\beta}_{\text{crime}}$, and $\hat{\beta}_{\text{pollution}}$, and can fail to detect genuine joint significance.

Conclusion: individual t-tests are not valid substitutes for the F-test when testing joint restrictions.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A model has $R^2_{ur} = 0.48$ with $k = 6$ regressors and $n = 200$. The restricted model (dropping the last 3 regressors) has $R^2_r = 0.42$. Compute the F-statistic for the joint significance of those 3 variables at the 5% level.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$q = 3$, $n - k - 1 = 200 - 6 - 1 = 193$.

$$F = \frac{(0.48 - 0.42)\,/\,3}{(1 - 0.48)\,/\,193} = \frac{0.06\,/\,3}{0.52\,/\,193} = \frac{0.020}{0.002694} = 7.42$$

Under $H_0$: $F \sim F_{3,\, 193}$. Critical value $F_{3,\, 193,\, 0.05} \approx 2.65$.

Since $7.42 > 2.65$, **reject $H_0$**: the three variables are jointly significant at 5%.
</details>
