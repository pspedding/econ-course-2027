## Explanation

### The Confidence Interval Formula

From Lesson M04.L01, we know that under MLR.1–MLR.6:

$$\frac{\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_j}{se(\hat{\beta}_j)} \sim t_{n-k-1}$$

A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for $\beta_j$ is constructed by inverting this pivotal quantity: find the values of $\beta_j$ that are **not** rejected by a two-sided $\alpha$-level t-test:

$$\boxed{\hat{\beta}_j \pm t_{n-k-1,\, \alpha/2} \cdot se(\hat{\beta}_j)}$$

where $t_{n-k-1,\, \alpha/2}$ is the upper $\alpha/2$ critical value of the $t_{n-k-1}$ distribution.

**Common confidence levels and their critical values (large $n$):**

| Confidence level | $\alpha$ | $\alpha/2$ | $t^*$ (large $n$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90% | 0.10 | 0.05 | 1.645 |
| 95% | 0.05 | 0.025 | 1.960 |
| 99% | 0.01 | 0.005 | 2.576 |

For finite samples, always use the exact $t_{n-k-1, \alpha/2}$ value from tables.

---

### Correct Interpretation

The correct frequentist interpretation of a 95% CI is:

> **If we were to repeat the sampling and estimation procedure many times, 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true value $\beta_j$.**

This is a statement about the **procedure**, not about any single realised interval.

**The parameter $\beta_j$ is a fixed (non-random) unknown constant.** It does not have a probability distribution. Once a specific interval $[L, U]$ has been computed from data, it either contains $\beta_j$ or it does not — there is no probability in between. The randomness lives entirely in $\hat{\beta}_j$ (and hence in $L$ and $U$), not in $\beta_j$.

---

### Relationship Between CIs and Hypothesis Tests

There is a direct duality between a two-sided t-test and a confidence interval:

$$\text{Reject } H_0: \beta_j = a_j \text{ at level } \alpha \iff a_j \notin \text{ } 100(1-\alpha)\% \text{ CI for } \beta_j$$

This means:
- If $0$ lies **outside** the 95% CI, we reject $H_0: \beta_j = 0$ at the 5% level.
- If $0$ lies **inside** the 95% CI, we fail to reject $H_0: \beta_j = 0$ at the 5% level.

The CI provides richer information than the hypothesis test — it tells you not just whether to reject, but also the **range of values consistent with the data** at the specified confidence level.

---

### What Makes a CI Wider or Narrower?

The width of a CI is $2 \cdot t_{n-k-1,\, \alpha/2} \cdot se(\hat{\beta}_j)$.

Since $se(\hat{\beta}_j) = \dfrac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{SST_j(1-R_j^2)}}$, the CI is wider when:

| Factor | Effect on CI width | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Smaller $n$ | **Wider** | Less data → less precision; also larger $t^*$ with fewer df |
| Larger $\hat{\sigma}^2$ (noisier data) | **Wider** | Regression errors are large |
| Less variation in $x_j$ (small $SST_j$) | **Wider** | Can't pin down slope without variation in regressor |
| High multicollinearity (large $R_j^2$) | **Wider** | $x_j$ is nearly explained by other regressors; little unique information |
| Higher confidence level (99% vs 95%) | **Wider** | Larger $t^*$ to capture more of the distribution |

## Worked Example

**Setup.** A graduate student estimates the following model of log earnings using $n = 935$ workers with $k = 4$ regressors ($df = 935 - 4 - 1 = 930$):

$$\log(\text{earnings}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{educ}_i + \beta_2 \text{exper}_i + \beta_3 \text{exper}^2_i + \beta_4 \text{female}_i + u_i$$

The OLS output is:

| Variable | $\hat{\beta}_j$ | $se(\hat{\beta}_j)$ |
|---|---|---|
| Intercept | 0.316 | 0.085 |
| educ | 0.078 | 0.006 |
| exper | 0.033 | 0.006 |
| exper² | −0.0005 | 0.0001 |
| female | −0.252 | 0.028 |

With $df = 930$, we use $t^* \approx 1.963$ for 95% CIs and $t^* \approx 2.581$ for 99% CIs (approximately equal to $z$ critical values given large $n$).

---

**95% CI for $\beta_1$ (return to education):**

$$\hat{\beta}_1 \pm t^* \cdot se(\hat{\beta}_1) = 0.078 \pm 1.963 \times 0.006$$
$$= 0.078 \pm 0.012$$
$$= (0.066,\; 0.090)$$

**Interpretation:** We are 95% confident that the true return to an additional year of education lies between 6.6% and 9.0% (log-point change in earnings), holding other variables constant.

Since 0 is far outside this interval, we reject $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ at the 5% level (consistent with $t = 0.078/0.006 = 13.0$, which is very large).

---

**99% CI for $\beta_4$ (gender gap):**

$$\hat{\beta}_4 \pm t^* \cdot se(\hat{\beta}_4) = -0.252 \pm 2.581 \times 0.028$$
$$= -0.252 \pm 0.072$$
$$= (-0.324,\; -0.180)$$

**Interpretation:** We are 99% confident that the female earnings penalty lies between $-32.4\%$ and $-18.0\%$ log earnings, controlling for education and experience. The entire interval lies below zero, confirming the gender gap is significant at the 1% level.

**Comparing 95% vs 99%:** The 99% CI for $\beta_4$ would be:

$$-0.252 \pm 2.581 \times 0.028 = (-0.324, -0.180)$$

vs the 95% CI:

$$-0.252 \pm 1.963 \times 0.028 = (-0.307, -0.197)$$

The 99% interval is wider by $2 \times (2.581 - 1.963) \times 0.028 = 2 \times 0.017 = 0.035$ — a modest widening given the large sample.

---

**CI for $\beta_2$ (experience, where $0$ might plausibly be inside):**

$$\hat{\beta}_2 \pm 1.963 \times 0.006 = 0.033 \pm 0.012 = (0.021,\; 0.045)$$

Zero is outside the interval, so we reject $H_0: \beta_2 = 0$ at 5%. But also note: zero is very far from 0.033 relative to $se = 0.006$. The experience effect is precisely estimated.

---

## Common Misconception

> **"There is a 95% probability that $\beta_j$ lies in the interval $(L, U)$."**

This is a common but incorrect statement. In the frequentist framework:

- $\beta_j$ is a **fixed unknown constant**, not a random variable.
- The interval endpoints $L = \hat{\beta}_j - t^* \cdot se(\hat{\beta}_j)$ and $U = \hat{\beta}_j + t^* \cdot se(\hat{\beta}_j)$ are **random** — they depend on the random sample.
- Once we observe the data and compute, say, $L = 0.066$ and $U = 0.090$, the probability that $\beta_1 \in (0.066, 0.090)$ is either 0 or 1 (it either does or doesn't). We just don't know which.

The correct statement: *"The interval was constructed by a procedure that, in repeated samples, would contain the true $\beta_j$ in 95% of cases."*

**Bayesian note:** A statement like "95% probability that $\beta_j \in (L, U)$" has a precise meaning in Bayesian statistics (it would be a 95% credible interval). But under frequentist OLS, this language is incorrect.

## Practice Prompts

**Q1.** A model of city crime rates has $n = 46$ cities, $k = 3$ regressors, $\hat{\beta}_{\text{unemployment}} = 0.82$, $se = 0.41$. Construct a 95% CI and a 90% CI. Does the 95% CI include zero? What does this imply for a two-sided test at 5%?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$df = 46 - 3 - 1 = 42$.

From t-tables: $t_{42, 0.025} \approx 2.018$ (95%), $t_{42, 0.05} \approx 1.682$ (90%).

**95% CI:** $0.82 \pm 2.018 \times 0.41 = 0.82 \pm 0.828 = (-0.008,\; 1.648)$.

**90% CI:** $0.82 \pm 1.682 \times 0.41 = 0.82 \pm 0.690 = (0.130,\; 1.510)$.

The 95% CI barely includes zero (lower bound $\approx -0.008$), so we **fail to reject** $H_0: \beta_{\text{unemployment}} = 0$ at the 5% level. However, the 90% CI excludes zero, so we **would reject** at the 10% level. The result is marginally insignificant at 5%: $t = 0.82/0.41 = 2.00 < t_{42,0.025} = 2.018$.
</details>

---

**Q2.** For the same regression, a colleague claims: "Because the 95% CI is $(-0.008, 1.648)$, there is only a 5% chance that the true effect is negative." Correct this statement.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The statement is incorrect. The parameter $\beta_{\text{unemployment}}$ is a fixed unknown — it is either positive or negative; there is no probability attached to its sign under frequentist inference.

The correct interpretation: the interval $(-0.008, 1.648)$ was produced by a procedure that yields intervals containing the true $\beta$ in 95% of repeated samples. We do not know whether this particular realised interval is one of the 95% that contain the truth, or one of the 5% that do not.

The fact that 95% of the interval lies in the positive region reflects the distribution of $\hat{\beta}$, not a probability over $\beta$.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A researcher reports: $\hat{\beta} = 0.120$, $se = 0.048$, $n = 80$, $k = 2$. Construct the 95% CI and verify that the result is consistent with the t-test outcome at the 5% level.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

$df = 80 - 2 - 1 = 77$. $t_{77, 0.025} \approx 1.991$.

**95% CI:** $0.120 \pm 1.991 \times 0.048 = 0.120 \pm 0.096 = (0.024,\; 0.216)$.

Zero is **outside** the interval, so we **reject** $H_0: \beta = 0$ at 5%.

**Verification via t-test:** $t = 0.120 / 0.048 = 2.50 > 1.991$. Reject $H_0$. ✓

Both approaches yield the same decision, confirming the duality between CIs and hypothesis tests.
</details>
