## Explanation

### Why Causality Matters

The goal of most applied econometrics is not prediction but **causal inference**: what is the effect of a policy, treatment, or variable $x$ on an outcome $y$? OLS recovers causal effects only under MLR.4 — zero conditional mean: $E(u \mid \mathbf{x}) = 0$. When this fails, we have **endogeneity**, and OLS does not estimate causal parameters even in very large samples.

---

### Definition: Endogeneity

A regressor $x_j$ is **endogenous** if:

$$\text{Cov}(x_j, u) \neq 0$$

This violates MLR.4 and means OLS is **biased and inconsistent** — the estimates do not converge to the true causal parameter even as $n \to \infty$.

---

### The Three Sources of Endogeneity

#### Source 1: Omitted Variables

The true model is:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 q + u$$

but $q$ is unobserved and omitted. The estimated model is:

$$y = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x + v, \qquad v = \beta_2 q + u$$

If $\text{Cov}(x, q) \neq 0$, then $\text{Cov}(x, v) \neq 0$ → $x$ is endogenous. The omitted variable bias formula (from M03) shows:

$$\text{plim}\,\hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \cdot \frac{\text{Cov}(x,q)}{\text{Var}(x)}$$

**Classic example:** Wage regressed on education alone. Ability ($q$) is omitted. Since ability is positively correlated with education and positively affects wages, the OLS coefficient on education is **upward biased**.

---

#### Source 2: Simultaneity (Reverse Causality)

Both $y$ and $x$ are jointly determined by a system of equations. The causal arrow runs in both directions:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u \quad \text{(demand equation)}$$
$$x = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 y + v \quad \text{(supply equation)}$$

**Classic example:** Price ($x$) and quantity ($y$) in a market. Price affects demand (quantity), but quantity demanded also affects price through market clearing. Since $x$ is partly determined by $y$, $\text{Cov}(x, u) \neq 0$ — OLS on either equation gives biased estimates of structural parameters.

**Another example:** Returns to schooling. Do smarter people go to school longer, or does school make people smarter? Both directions may be present, creating a simultaneous relationship between education and wages.

---

#### Source 3: Measurement Error in $x$

Suppose the true model has the *true* regressor $x^*$ but we observe a **mismeasured** $x = x^* + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon$ is classical measurement error ($\text{Cov}(\epsilon, x^*) = 0$, $\text{Cov}(\epsilon, u) = 0$).

The estimated model uses $x$ instead of $x^*$:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + (u - \beta_1 \epsilon)$$

The composite error is $(u - \beta_1 \epsilon)$, which is correlated with $x = x^* + \epsilon$ through $\epsilon$. This creates **attenuation bias**:

$$\text{plim}\,\hat{\beta}_1 = \beta_1 \cdot \frac{\text{Var}(x^*)}{\text{Var}(x^*) + \text{Var}(\epsilon)} = \beta_1 \cdot \left(1 - \frac{\text{Var}(\epsilon)}{\text{Var}(x)}\right) < \beta_1 \text{ (if } \beta_1 > 0\text{)}$$

OLS is **attenuated toward zero** — underestimates the true effect. This is the **errors-in-variables** problem.

---

### Probability Limit of OLS Under Endogeneity

For the simple regression $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u$, the probability limit of $\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{OLS}}$ is:

$$\text{plim}\,\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{OLS}} = \beta_1 + \frac{\text{Cov}(x, u)}{\text{Var}(x)}$$

The bias is:

$$\text{Inconsistency} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, u)}{\text{Var}(x)}$$

**Key properties:**

- If $\text{Cov}(x,u) > 0$: OLS **overestimates** $\beta_1$ (upward inconsistency)  
- If $\text{Cov}(x,u) < 0$: OLS **underestimates** $\beta_1$ (downward inconsistency)  
- If $\text{Cov}(x,u) = 0$: OLS is consistent (even if $u$ is not zero-mean, as long as the intercept absorbs it)

---

### Classic Examples of Endogeneity

| Model | Endogenous variable | Source | Direction of OLS bias |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wage = f(education) | education | Ability in $u$ (omitted) | Upward |
| Test scores = f(class size) | class size | School resources (omitted); richer schools have smaller classes AND better outcomes | Downward (class size coefficient is biased toward zero or negative) |
| Crime = f(police) | police | More crime → more police (simultaneity) | Upward (more police appear to increase crime) |
| Earnings = f(health) | health | Income enables better health (simultaneity) | Upward |
| Sales = f(price) | price | Demand and supply simultaneous | Ambiguous |

## Worked Example

**Problem:** Estimate the effect of class size on student test scores.

$$\text{score}_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{class\_size}_t + u_t$$

**Why is class size endogenous?**

Wealthy school districts tend to:
1. Have smaller class sizes (more per-pupil spending)
2. Have students from higher-income families → higher scores

The omitted variable "school district wealth" is positively correlated with scores and negatively correlated with class size ($\text{Cov}(\text{class\_size}, u) < 0$). The bias:

$$\text{plim}\,\hat{\beta}_1 = \beta_1 + \frac{\text{Cov}(\text{class\_size}, u)}{\text{Var}(\text{class\_size})} = \beta_1 + \text{negative number}$$

OLS will **overstate the negative effect** of class size (i.e., make smaller classes look more beneficial than they truly are). This is a case of negative bias when the true $\beta_1 < 0$.

**Magnitude illustration:**

Suppose the true causal effect of a 1-student increase in class size on scores is $\beta_1 = -0.5$ points. If $\text{Cov}(\text{class\_size}, u)/\text{Var}(\text{class\_size}) = -0.8$:

$$\text{plim}\,\hat{\beta}_1 = -0.5 + (-0.8) = -1.3$$

OLS reports $-1.3$ — class size appears 2.6× more harmful than it truly is. A policy maker who reduces class size by 10 students would predict a 13-point gain, but the true causal gain is only 5 points.

**Implication:** Any study that doesn't account for school wealth/resources in the error term will give misleading policy guidance.

---

## Common Misconception

> **"Using a large sample fixes endogeneity — the law of large numbers takes care of it."**

**This is false.** Consistency requires $\text{Cov}(x, u) = 0$, not just a large $n$. If $\text{Cov}(x, u) = c \neq 0$, the OLS estimator converges as $n \to \infty$ to:

$$\text{plim}\,\hat{\beta}_1 = \beta_1 + \frac{c}{\text{Var}(x)} \neq \beta_1$$

A larger sample narrows the confidence interval around a **wrong** estimate. With $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ and endogeneity, you get a very precise, very wrong answer. The law of large numbers ensures convergence — it just converges to the wrong limit.

**Analogy:** A scale that reads 2 kg too heavy is biased. Weighing yourself 10,000 times averages to "2 kg too heavy" — you don't get the correct weight, you just confirm the wrong answer with great precision.

## Practice Prompts

1. A researcher estimates returns to schooling using OLS with $n = 50{,}000$ observations, obtaining $\hat{\beta} = 0.12$ (12% wage increase per year of school). They argue that with such a large sample, endogeneity concerns are irrelevant. Critique this argument using the probability limit formula.

2. Consider the model $\text{crime}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{police}_{it} + u_{it}$ estimated by OLS. Explain the simultaneity problem. Is the expected OLS bias positive or negative relative to the true effect of police on crime? (Hint: what does city government do when crime rises?)

3. A firm measures employee productivity $y$ using self-reported hours worked $x$, which suffers from classical measurement error. If the true elasticity is $\beta_1 = 0.8$ and the reliability ratio $\text{Var}(x^*)/\text{Var}(x) = 0.7$, compute the probability limit of the OLS estimator. Is this attenuation bias economically significant?
