## Explanation

### Time Series Assumptions: TS.1′–TS.5′

In cross-section, OLS requires **strict exogeneity** (MLR.4): $E(u_i \mid \mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n) = 0$. In time series, strict exogeneity is often implausible — it rules out lagged dependent variables and feedback from $y$ to future $x$. The **relaxed** assumptions TS.1′–TS.5′ replace strict with **contemporaneous exogeneity**:

| Assumption | Statement |
|---|---|
| **TS.1′** | Linearity in parameters (same as before) |
| **TS.2′** | No perfect collinearity among regressors |
| **TS.3′** | **Contemporaneous exogeneity**: $E(u_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = 0$ — the error is mean-zero given *current* regressors only |
| **TS.4′** | **Homoskedasticity**: $\text{Var}(u_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = \sigma^2$ |
| **TS.5′** | **No serial correlation**: $\text{Cov}(u_t, u_s \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_s) = 0$ for $t \neq s$ |

Under **TS.1′–TS.3′** alone, OLS is **consistent** for $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. Under **TS.1′–TS.5′**, OLS is BLUE and standard $t$- and $F$-statistics are valid.

**Key distinction:**  
- Strict exogeneity → OLS unbiased (stronger, harder to satisfy)  
- Contemporaneous exogeneity → OLS consistent (weaker, sufficient for large-sample validity *if* TS.5′ also holds)

### What Happens When TS.5′ Fails?

When $\text{Cov}(u_t, u_s) \neq 0$, **serial correlation** is present. Under TS.1′–TS.3′ (contemporaneous exogeneity), OLS is **still consistent** — the coefficient estimates converge to the true $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ as $n \to \infty$. But the **OLS standard errors are wrong** because their derivation assumes TS.5′.

> **Serial correlation does NOT bias OLS estimates (under contemporaneous exogeneity). It only invalidates inference.**

### AR(1) Error Process

The simplest and most common serial correlation structure is the **first-order autoregressive** process:

$$u_t = \rho\, u_{t-1} + e_t, \qquad e_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, \sigma_e^2), \quad |\rho| < 1$$

- $\rho$ is the **autocorrelation coefficient** of the errors  
- $e_t$ is a white-noise innovation — uncorrelated with everything up to period $t-1$  
- Stationarity requires $|\rho| < 1$

The variance and autocovariances of $u_t$ under AR(1):

$$\text{Var}(u_t) = \frac{\sigma_e^2}{1 - \rho^2}, \qquad \text{Cov}(u_t, u_{t-s}) = \rho^s \cdot \frac{\sigma_e^2}{1 - \rho^2}$$

The autocorrelations decay geometrically:

$$\text{Corr}(u_t, u_{t-s}) = \rho^s$$

### Effect of Serial Correlation on OLS Variance

Consider a simple time-series regression $y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_t + u_t$ with AR(1) errors. The **true** asymptotic variance of $\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{OLS}}$ is larger than the standard OLS formula assumes.

Under positive serial correlation ($\rho > 0$):

$$\text{True Var}(\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{OLS}}) > \text{Computed Var}_{\text{OLS}}(\hat{\beta}_1)$$

**Intuition:** Positively correlated errors mean consecutive observations carry less independent information than $n$ i.i.d. draws. The "effective sample size" is smaller than $n$, but the standard OLS SE formula pretends it equals $n$ — so it **understates** the true variance.

Consequences of understated SEs when $\rho > 0$:

| Statistic | Effect |
|---|---|
| Standard errors | **Too small** |
| $t$-statistics | **Too large** (inflated) |
| Confidence intervals | **Too narrow** |
| Hypothesis tests | **Over-reject $H_0$** — spurious significance |

#### Why Positive Serial Correlation is Common in Economics

Economic time series exhibit persistence for structural reasons:

- **Technology shocks** take time to diffuse through the economy  
- **Policy changes** (monetary, fiscal) have lagged multiplier effects  
- **Demand shocks** (oil prices, consumer confidence) propagate through supply chains  
- **Habit formation** in consumption: $c_t$ depends on $c_{t-1}$  

Positive $\rho$ is the rule rather than the exception in macro time series.

## Worked Example

**Setup:** Consider a monthly model of consumer spending on income:

$$\text{cons}_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{income}_t + u_t$$

Suppose the true error process is AR(1) with $\rho = 0.6$, $\sigma_e^2 = 1$, $n = 120$, and $\text{Var}(x) = 4$.

**Step 1: True error variance**

$$\text{Var}(u_t) = \frac{\sigma_e^2}{1-\rho^2} = \frac{1}{1-0.36} = \frac{1}{0.64} = 1.5625$$

**Step 2: OLS-computed variance of $\hat{\beta}_1$** (incorrectly assuming i.i.d. errors)

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}_{\text{OLS}}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{\sum(x_t - \bar{x})^2} \approx \frac{1.5625}{n \cdot \text{Var}(x)} = \frac{1.5625}{120 \times 4} = 0.003255$$

$$\widehat{\text{SE}}_{\text{OLS}}(\hat{\beta}_1) \approx 0.057$$

**Step 3: True asymptotic variance** (accounting for AR(1) correlation)

The true variance involves a correction factor. For a simple illustration, the ratio of true to OLS variance under AR(1) is approximately:

$$\frac{\text{True Var}}{\text{OLS Var}} \approx 1 + 2\rho \cdot r_x$$

where $r_x = \text{Corr}(x_t, x_{t-1})$. If $r_x = 0.5$ (income is also persistent):

$$\text{Inflation factor} \approx 1 + 2(0.6)(0.5) = 1.6$$

$$\text{True SE} \approx 0.057 \times \sqrt{1.6} = 0.057 \times 1.265 \approx 0.072$$

**Step 4: Interpret**

The OLS standard error (0.057) is **21% smaller** than the true standard error (0.072). If the true $t$-statistic were $0.072/\text{s.e.} = 1.8$, the OLS procedure would report $t = 0.057^{-1} \times \hat{\beta}_1$ and classify it as significant at 5% when it should not be. This is the mechanism of **spurious significance** due to serial correlation.

## Common Misconception

> **"Serial correlation biases OLS coefficient estimates."**

**This is false under contemporaneous exogeneity.** If $E(u_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = 0$ (TS.3′), OLS is consistent regardless of serial correlation in $u_t$. The coefficients converge to the true $\beta$ in large samples. The *only* problem is that the OLS standard errors are computed under the false assumption that errors are uncorrelated — so inference (t-tests, confidence intervals, F-tests) is invalid.

**The correct statement:** Serial correlation leaves OLS point estimates consistent but renders inference unreliable. The fix is corrected standard errors (HAC) or GLS — not a new estimation strategy to unbias $\hat{\beta}$.

## Practice Prompts

1. A researcher estimates a quarterly GDP growth regression and finds $\hat{\rho} = 0.7$ from the residuals. The OLS $t$-statistic on the lagged policy variable is 2.4. Should the researcher conclude the policy variable is significant at the 5% level? Explain your reasoning in terms of what $\hat{\rho} = 0.7$ implies for the OLS SE.

2. Under AR(1) errors with $\rho = -0.4$ (negative serial correlation), in which direction are OLS standard errors biased — understated or overstated? What does this imply for Type I and Type II errors in hypothesis testing?

3. Consider the regression $\log(\text{wage}_t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{edu}_t + u_t$ estimated on annual panel data. Explain why contemporaneous exogeneity (TS.3′) is a more defensible assumption than strict exogeneity for this model. Does contemporaneous exogeneity alone guarantee valid OLS $t$-statistics?
