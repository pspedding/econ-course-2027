## Explanation

### Variance of $\hat{\beta}_j$ in Multiple Regression

Under **MLR.1–MLR.5**, the sampling variance of the OLS estimator for $\beta_j$ is:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_j \cdot (1 - R_j^2)}$$

where:

| Symbol | Definition |
|--------|-----------|
| $\sigma^2$ | Population error variance ($\text{Var}(u \mid \mathbf{x})$) |
| $SST_j$ | $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2$ — total sample variation in $x_j$ |
| $R_j^2$ | $R^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on **all other** regressors $x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_k$ |

The standard error (square root of estimated variance) is:

$$\widehat{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{SST_j \cdot (1 - R_j^2)}}$$

where $\hat{\sigma}^2 = SSR/(n-k-1)$.

---

### Three Ways to Get Small Variance

From the formula, $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ is small when:

1. **$\sigma^2$ is small** — the regression model fits well; not much unexplained variation in $y$
2. **$SST_j$ is large** — plenty of variation in $x_j$ in the sample; more signal
3. **$R_j^2$ is small** — $x_j$ is not well explained by the other regressors; no multicollinearity

The third factor is what makes multicollinearity costly.

---

### Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)

Define the **Variance Inflation Factor** for $\hat{\beta}_j$:

$$VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

So:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_j} \cdot VIF_j$$

The factor $\sigma^2/SST_j$ is what the variance would be if $x_j$ were **orthogonal** to all other regressors ($R_j^2 = 0$, so $VIF_j = 1$). Multicollinearity inflates this baseline by the factor $VIF_j \geq 1$.

**Rule of thumb:**

| $VIF_j$ | Interpretation |
|---------|---------------|
| $1$ | No collinearity; baseline variance |
| $1$–$5$ | Mild; generally acceptable |
| $5$–$10$ | Moderate; monitor closely |
| $> 10$ | Severe; consider remedies |

Some textbooks use VIF $> 10$ as the threshold; others use VIF $> 5$. Judgement and context matter.

---

### Perfect Multicollinearity (MLR.3 Violated)

**Perfect multicollinearity:** $R_j^2 = 1$ — one regressor is an *exact* linear combination of others.

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_j \cdot (1 - 1)} = \frac{\sigma^2}{0} = \infty$$

OLS **breaks down completely** — the matrix $\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}$ is singular and $(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ does not exist. Software will drop one of the collinear columns or throw an error.

**Common causes:**

- **Dummy variable trap:** Including $d_{\text{male}}$ and $d_{\text{female}}$ with an intercept (they sum to 1)
- **Exact definitional relationship:** Including both `total_tax` and `income_tax + GST + other_tax` when the latter three sum exactly to the former
- **Redundant transformation:** Including $x$ and $2x$ simultaneously

---

### Imperfect Multicollinearity

**Imperfect multicollinearity:** $R_j^2$ close to (but not equal to) 1.

OLS is still **computable, unbiased, and BLUE** — MLR.3 is not violated. However:

- $VIF_j$ is large → $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ is large
- Standard errors are large
- $t$-statistics are small → it is hard to detect that $\beta_j \neq 0$ even when it truly is (Type II error)
- Coefficient estimates are sensitive to small changes in the sample (overfitting)

The core problem: with highly correlated regressors, the data contain **insufficient independent information** to precisely estimate the separate effects.

---

### Economic Causes of Multicollinearity

1. **Aggregate macroeconomic variables:** GDP, consumption, and investment all trend together — high pairwise correlations in time series.

2. **Overlapping demographic controls:** Age, years of experience, and years since leaving school are all highly correlated life-stage variables.

3. **Physical characteristics:** Height and weight are highly correlated for human subjects.

4. **Lagged variables:** Including $x_t$, $x_{t-1}$, $x_{t-2}$ in a time-series model creates near-perfect collinearity if $x$ is a smooth series.

---

### Remedies for Multicollinearity

| Remedy | How | Caution |
|--------|-----|---------|
| **Collect more data** | Larger $n$ reduces $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ directly | Not always feasible; may not solve collinearity if correlation is structural |
| **Drop a variable** | Remove one collinear regressor | Only valid if the dropped variable *doesn't belong* — otherwise introduces OVB |
| **Principal components** | Replace correlated $x$'s with uncorrelated linear combinations | Components lose economic interpretability |
| **Ridge regression** | Add penalty $\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2$ to SSR | Introduces bias; requires choice of tuning parameter $\lambda$ |

> **Note:** If the collinear variable *belongs* in the model (non-zero $\beta_j$), dropping it trades multicollinearity for OVB — often a worse problem. The preferred solution is more data.

## Worked Example

**Setup:** A researcher regresses log-housing prices on three regressors: $x_1$ = floor area (m²), $x_2$ = number of bedrooms, $x_3$ = number of bathrooms. All are likely correlated.

**Auxiliary regressions** (to compute $R_j^2$):

| $j$ | Regress $x_j$ on others | $R_j^2$ | $VIF_j$ |
|-----|------------------------|---------|---------|
| 1 (floor area) | $x_2, x_3$ | 0.82 | $1/(1-0.82) = 5.56$ |
| 2 (bedrooms) | $x_1, x_3$ | 0.75 | $1/(1-0.75) = 4.00$ |
| 3 (bathrooms) | $x_1, x_2$ | 0.88 | $1/(1-0.88) = 8.33$ |

**Baseline variance** (if $R_j^2 = 0$):

Suppose $\hat{\sigma}^2 = 0.04$ and $SST_3 = 200$ (variation in bathrooms). Then:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_3)_{\text{baseline}} = \frac{0.04}{200} = 0.0002$$

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_3) = 0.0002 \times VIF_3 = 0.0002 \times 8.33 = 0.001666$$

$$SE(\hat{\beta}_3) = \sqrt{0.001666} \approx 0.0408 \quad \text{vs baseline } \sqrt{0.0002} \approx 0.0141$$

The standard error for $\hat{\beta}_3$ is **2.89 times larger** than it would be with no multicollinearity (since $\sqrt{VIF_3} = \sqrt{8.33} \approx 2.89$). This makes the $t$-statistic for $\beta_3$ roughly 2.89 times smaller — harder to achieve statistical significance even if bathrooms genuinely affect price.

**Diagnosis:** $VIF_3 = 8.33$ is in the "moderate to severe" range. The researcher should check whether floor area and bathrooms are nearly perfectly collinear, possibly because large homes always have many bathrooms. Adding the number of floors or age of building as an alternative dimension of heterogeneity may help.

## Common Misconception

!!! warning "Multicollinearity biases OLS estimates"
    **False.** Under MLR.1–MLR.4, OLS is **unbiased regardless of multicollinearity** (as long as MLR.3 holds, i.e., no *perfect* collinearity). High $R_j^2$ inflates $\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ and standard errors — affecting *efficiency* and *inference* — but **does not bias** $E(\hat{\beta}_j)$. Confusing multicollinearity with OVB is one of the most common errors in applied econometrics.

!!! warning "Dropping a correlated variable fixes multicollinearity without cost"
    Dropping a variable reduces multicollinearity but only "fixes" the problem costlessly if the dropped variable has $\beta_j = 0$ in the true model. If $\beta_j \neq 0$, you have traded a precision problem (large SE) for a bias problem (OVB) — and bias is generally worse because it cannot be fixed by collecting more data.

## The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) Theorem

The FWL theorem reveals the exact geometric meaning of "controlling for other variables" in multiple regression — one of the most important insights in applied econometrics.

**Theorem:** In the regression $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u$, the OLS estimate $\hat{\beta}_1$ is numerically identical to the slope from the simple regression of $\tilde{y}$ on $\tilde{x}_1$, where:

$$\tilde{x}_1 = M_2 x_1, \qquad \tilde{y} = M_2 y$$

and $M_2 = I - X_2(X_2'X_2)^{-1}X_2'$ is the **residual maker** that projects out $x_2$ (and the constant).

$$\hat{\beta}_1^{\text{MLR}} = \frac{\sum \tilde{x}_{1i} \tilde{y}_i}{\sum \tilde{x}_{1i}^2}$$

**In plain words:**

- $\tilde{x}_{1i}$ = the part of $x_1$ *not explained by* $x_2$ (residuals from regressing $x_1$ on $x_2$)
- $\tilde{y}_i$ = the part of $y$ *not explained by* $x_2$ (residuals from regressing $y$ on $x_2$)
- $\hat{\beta}_1$ = slope relating these two "cleaned" variables

This is literally and algebraically what "holding $x_2$ constant" means.

### Worked Example — Wages, Education, Experience

Suppose we regress $\ln(\text{wage})$ on education ($\text{educ}$) and experience ($\text{exper}$):

$$\ln(\text{wage}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{educ} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{exper} + u$$

By FWL, $\hat{\beta}_\text{educ}$ can be computed as:

1. Regress $\text{educ}$ on $\text{exper}$: $\text{educ} = \delta_0 + \delta_1 \cdot \text{exper} + v$ → get residuals $\tilde{\text{educ}}_i$
2. Regress $\ln(\text{wage})$ on $\text{exper}$: get residuals $\tilde{y}_i$
3. Simple regression $\tilde{y}_i$ on $\tilde{\text{educ}}_i$: slope $= \hat{\beta}_\text{educ}$ (identical to multiple regression)

$\tilde{\text{educ}}_i$ captures variation in education that is **orthogonal to experience** — education differences that cannot be explained by differences in experience. This is the "clean" variation the multiple regression uses to estimate the education return.

!!! tip "Why FWL matters for ECMT5001"
    FWL explains exactly *why* adding a control variable changes a coefficient. If education and experience are positively correlated, and experience also raises wages ($\beta_2 > 0$), then the simple regression of wages on education **overstates** the education return (upward OVB). Multiple regression removes this bias by using only $\tilde{\text{educ}}_i$ — variation in education orthogonal to experience — so the estimated $\hat{\beta}_\text{educ}$ is no longer contaminated by the experience channel.

    Any ECMT5001 exam question asking "what does controlling for X mean?" or "why does the coefficient change when you add Z?" is fundamentally asking about the FWL mechanism.

!!! info "Source"
    Frisch & Waugh (1933), Lovell (1963). Proven in Heinze (2026, Ch. 1.3.8) and Wooldridge (Appendix E). Generalises to any number of partialled-out variables.
