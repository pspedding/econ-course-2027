## Explanation

### What Is Omitted Variable Bias?

**Omitted variable bias (OVB)** arises when a variable that *belongs* in the true regression — because it affects $y$ *and* correlates with at least one included regressor — is left out. The OLS estimate of the included regressor's coefficient absorbs part of the omitted variable's effect and is therefore biased.

OVB is a violation of **MLR.4**: the omitted variable ends up in the error term $u$, making $E(u \mid x_1) \neq 0$.

---

### The OVB Formula (Two-Variable Case)

**True model (population):**

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u, \quad E(u \mid x_1, x_2) = 0$$

**Estimated model (researcher omits $x_2$):**

$$y = \tilde{\beta}_0 + \tilde{\beta}_1 x_1 + v, \quad v = \beta_2 x_2 + u$$

The simple-regression OLS estimator of $\tilde{\beta}_1$ is:

$$\tilde{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1)(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1)^2}$$

Taking expectations (using the true model):

$$E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \tilde{\delta}_1$$

where $\tilde{\delta}_1$ is the **auxiliary regression coefficient** — the slope from regressing $x_2$ on $x_1$:

$$\tilde{\delta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1)(x_{i2} - \bar{x}_2)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1)^2}$$

!!! note "Key formula"
    $$\boxed{E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \underbrace{\beta_2 \cdot \tilde{\delta}_1}_{\text{OVB}}}$$
    
    Bias $= \beta_2 \cdot \tilde{\delta}_1$

---

### Direction of Bias

The sign of OVB depends on the **signs of $\beta_2$ and $\tilde{\delta}_1$**:

| $\beta_2 > 0$ (omitted var ↑ $y$) | $\beta_2 < 0$ (omitted var ↓ $y$) |
|----------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| $\tilde{\delta}_1 > 0$ (omitted corr. positively with $x_1$) → **Upward bias** | $\tilde{\delta}_1 > 0$ → **Downward bias** |
| $\tilde{\delta}_1 < 0$ (omitted corr. negatively with $x_1$) → **Downward bias** | $\tilde{\delta}_1 < 0$ → **Upward bias** |

Mnemonic: **Same signs → upward bias; opposite signs → downward bias**

Note: "upward bias" means $E(\tilde{\beta}_1) > \beta_1$ (OLS *over*estimates the true effect).

---

### Classic Example: Ability Bias in Returns to Education

**True model:**

$$\ln(\text{wage}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{educ} + \beta_2\,\text{ability} + u$$

- $\beta_2 > 0$: more able workers earn higher wages (ability raises $y$)
- $\tilde{\delta}_1 > 0$: more able individuals tend to acquire more education (ability correlates positively with educ)

**OVB:**

$$E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \underbrace{\beta_2}_{>0} \cdot \underbrace{\tilde{\delta}_1}_{>0} > \beta_1$$

**Conclusion:** Simple OLS of log-wage on education **overestimates** the true return to education. Part of what we attribute to schooling is really the wage premium for ability.

---

### Australian Example: Family Background and Returns to Education

In Australia, children from wealthier family backgrounds tend to:
1. Achieve higher educational attainment (`educ` ↑)
2. Earn higher wages due to networks, cultural capital, and inherited advantages (`wage` ↑)

If a researcher regresses $\ln(\text{wage})$ on `educ` without controlling for family background (SES index):

- $\beta_2 > 0$ (SES raises wages)
- $\tilde{\delta}_1 > 0$ (SES correlates positively with education)

→ Upward OVB on $\hat{\beta}_1$. The estimated return to education is inflated.

This has direct policy implications: if we overestimate returns to education, we may over-invest in tertiary subsidies relative to other interventions.

---

### Worked Example: Calculate Direction and Magnitude

**Setup:** A researcher has data on 500 workers and estimates:

$$\widehat{\ln(\text{wage})} = 0.50 + 0.12\,\text{educ} \qquad (\text{simple regression})$$

An econometrician reveals that the true model is:

$$\ln(\text{wage}) = 0.20 + 0.08\,\text{educ} + 0.04\,\text{ability} + u$$

The auxiliary regression of `ability` on `educ` gives:

$$\widehat{\text{ability}} = -1.2 + 0.9\,\text{educ} \implies \tilde{\delta}_1 = 0.9$$

**Step 1: Compute predicted OVB**

$$\text{OVB} = \beta_2 \cdot \tilde{\delta}_1 = 0.04 \times 0.9 = 0.036$$

**Step 2: Check against formula**

$$E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \text{OVB} = 0.08 + 0.036 = 0.116$$

This is close to the observed $\tilde{\beta}_1 = 0.12$ (small discrepancy due to sampling variation). ✓

**Step 3: Direction of bias**

$\beta_2 = 0.04 > 0$ and $\tilde{\delta}_1 = 0.9 > 0$ → **upward bias**. The simple regression overestimates the return to education by roughly $0.036$ log-wage units (≈ 3.6 percentage points).

**Policy implication:** A policy that raises education by 1 year would produce an approximately 8% wage increase (true $\beta_1 = 0.08$), not the 12% suggested by the biased estimate.

---

### OVB with Multiple Included Regressors

In the general case with multiple included regressors $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ and omitted variable $x_{k+1}$:

$$\text{OVB on }\hat{\beta}_j = \beta_{k+1} \cdot \tilde{\delta}_j$$

where $\tilde{\delta}_j$ is the coefficient on $x_j$ in the auxiliary regression of $x_{k+1}$ on all included $x_1,\ldots,x_k$. Every included coefficient is potentially biased if the omitted variable correlates with it.

---

## Common Misconception

!!! warning "Adding more variables always eliminates OVB"
    **Partially true, but dangerous.** Adding the *correct* omitted variable eliminates OVB. However:
    
    1. **The added variable may itself be endogenous** — then you introduce a *new* form of bias. For example, adding job type to control for ability may be a bad control if job type is partly *caused* by education.
    
    2. **Proxy variables** (e.g., IQ test scores as a proxy for ability) reduce but do not eliminate OVB, because the proxy is an imperfect measure.
    
    3. **Adding irrelevant variables** does not reduce any bias but inflates variance and can induce new near-multicollinearity.

!!! warning "Zero correlation means no OVB"
    If the omitted variable is **uncorrelated with $x_1$** ($\tilde{\delta}_1 = 0$), then $\text{OVB} = \beta_2 \cdot 0 = 0$ even if $\beta_2 \neq 0$. A variable that affects $y$ but is uncorrelated with included regressors goes into the error term without biasing any coefficient — it only increases residual variance ($\hat{\sigma}^2$) and hence standard errors.

## Practice Prompts

1. **OVB direction.** A researcher estimates the effect of the minimum wage (`minwage`) on employment (`emp`) without controlling for regional economic conditions (`local_gdp`). Suppose the true model is:
   
   $$\ln(\text{emp}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(\text{minwage}) + \beta_2 \ln(\text{local\_gdp}) + u$$
   
   with $\beta_2 > 0$. Australian states with higher local GDP tend to set higher minimum wages ($\tilde{\delta}_1 > 0$). Without controlling for `local_gdp`, is the OLS estimate of $\beta_1$ (the employment elasticity of the minimum wage) biased upward or downward? What does this imply about published estimates of minimum wage effects that don't control for regional conditions?

2. **Derive OVB.** Start from the true model $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u$ and the formula $\tilde{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum(x_{i1}-\bar{x}_1)(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum(x_{i1}-\bar{x}_1)^2}$. Substitute the true model for $y_i$ and show that $E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \beta_2\tilde{\delta}_1$.

3. **Numerical OVB.** True model: $y = 1 + 2x_1 + 3x_2 + u$. Auxiliary regression: $x_2 = 0.5 + 0.4x_1 + \epsilon$. You estimate the misspecified model $y = \tilde{\beta}_0 + \tilde{\beta}_1 x_1 + v$. (a) Compute $E(\tilde{\beta}_1)$. (b) Is OLS upward or downward biased? (c) How much does OLS overstate/understate the effect of $x_1$?
