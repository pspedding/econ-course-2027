## Explanation

### OLS in Multiple Regression: The Objective

Given the model $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u$, OLS chooses estimates $\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1, \ldots, \hat{\beta}_k$ to **minimise the sum of squared residuals (SSR)**:

$$\min_{\hat{\beta}_0,\ldots,\hat{\beta}_k} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{u}_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}\bigr)^2$$

In simple regression (one regressor) the first-order conditions give explicit formulas. With $k > 1$ regressors the FOCs are a system of $k+1$ equations — solved most cleanly in matrix notation.

---

### Matrix Notation (Awareness Level)

Stack the data into matrices:

$$\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix}y_1\\\vdots\\y_n\end{pmatrix}_{n\times 1}, \quad \mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1k}\\\vdots & & & \vdots\\1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{nk}\end{pmatrix}_{n\times(k+1)}, \quad \boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_k\end{pmatrix}_{(k+1)\times 1}$$

The OLS estimator in closed form is:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

**Requirements for this formula to exist:**

- $(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})$ must be **invertible** — equivalent to MLR.3 (no perfect collinearity)
- $n > k + 1$ — more observations than parameters

You are not expected to derive this result from scratch in ECMT5001, but you should recognise the notation and understand that the scalar formulas for $k=1$ are a special case.

---

### Fitted Values and Residuals

Once $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is obtained:

$$\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}$$

$$\hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$$

These definitions are identical to simple regression — the OLS residual is always the actual minus fitted value.

---

### Algebraic Properties of OLS

These properties follow mechanically from the first-order conditions and hold for **any** OLS regression (simple or multiple):

| Property | Statement |
|----------|-----------|
| **Zero mean residuals** | $\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i = 0$ |
| **Orthogonality** | $\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij}\hat{u}_i = 0$ for each $j = 1,\ldots,k$ |
| **Sample means on surface** | $(\bar{x}_1, \ldots, \bar{x}_k, \bar{y})$ lies on the OLS regression surface |
| **SST decomposition** | $SST = SSE + SSR$ where $SSE = \sum(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2$ |

The orthogonality conditions say the residuals are uncorrelated with every included regressor in the sample. This is a mechanical property — it does *not* imply $E(u|x_j) = 0$ in the population (that requires Assumption MLR.4).

---

### $R^2$ in Multiple Regression

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST} = \frac{SSE}{SST}$$

This is identical to the simple-regression formula. $R^2$ is the fraction of the total variation in $y$ explained by all regressors jointly.

**Key mechanical fact:** $R^2$ **never decreases** when you add a regressor. Why? OLS can always set the new coefficient to zero and reproduce the old fit; any nonzero coefficient can only reduce SSR further.

This makes $R^2$ a poor model-selection criterion — it will always favour the kitchen-sink model.

---

### Adjusted $R^2$ (Penalised Fit)

To penalise for adding extra parameters, define:

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{SSR/(n-k-1)}{SST/(n-1)} = 1 - \frac{n-1}{n-k-1}(1 - R^2)$$

| Component | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| $SSR/(n-k-1)$ | Estimated residual variance $\hat{\sigma}^2$ — penalises for each parameter used |
| $SST/(n-1)$ | Sample variance of $y$ — fixed |

**Properties of $\bar{R}^2$:**

- Can be **negative** (when the regression fits worse than the intercept-only model)
- **Decreases** when an added variable contributes less than its "fair share" to fit reduction
- $\bar{R}^2 \leq R^2$ always (equality when $k=0$)
- A regressor raises $\bar{R}^2$ if and only if its $|t\text{-stat}| > 1$ (approximately)

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{s_y^2}$$

where $s_y^2 = SST/(n-1)$ is the sample variance of $y$. This framing makes clear: adding a regressor raises $\bar{R}^2$ iff it reduces the estimated residual variance $\hat{\sigma}^2$.

---

### Which to Report?

- **$R^2$:** useful for describing how much variation is explained; always report it.
- **$\bar{R}^2$:** use for comparing models with **different numbers of regressors** on the **same sample and same $y$**.
- Neither is appropriate for comparing models with different transformations of $y$ (e.g., $\ln y$ vs $y$), because $SST$ changes.

## Worked Example

A researcher estimates two models on $n = 100$ observations of Australian workers:

**Model 1:** $\widehat{\ln(\text{wage})} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1\,\text{educ}$

| Statistic | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| $SSR_1$ | 48.6 |
| $SST$ | 72.0 |
| $R^2$ | 0.325 |
| $\bar{R}^2$ | ? |

**Model 2:** $\widehat{\ln(\text{wage})} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1\,\text{educ} + \hat{\beta}_2\,\text{exper} + \hat{\beta}_3\,\text{tenure}$

| Statistic | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| $SSR_2$ | 40.3 |
| $SST$ | 72.0 |
| $R^2$ | 0.440 |
| $\bar{R}^2$ | ? |

**Step 1: Compute $\bar{R}^2$ for Model 1** ($k=1$, $n=100$)

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{48.6/98}{72.0/99} = 1 - \frac{0.4959}{0.7273} = 1 - 0.682 = 0.318$$

**Step 2: Compute $\bar{R}^2$ for Model 2** ($k=3$, $n=100$)

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{40.3/96}{72.0/99} = 1 - \frac{0.4198}{0.7273} = 1 - 0.577 = 0.423$$

**Step 3: Compare**

| Model | $k$ | $R^2$ | $\bar{R}^2$ |
|-------|-----|-------|-------------|
| 1 (educ only) | 1 | 0.325 | 0.318 |
| 2 (educ + exper + tenure) | 3 | 0.440 | 0.423 |

Both $R^2$ and $\bar{R}^2$ rise from Model 1 to Model 2, confirming that experience and tenure are jointly contributing meaningfully to explained variation — they are not just soaking up degrees of freedom.

**Step 4: Compute $\hat{\sigma}^2$ for Model 2**

$$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{SSR}{n-k-1} = \frac{40.3}{96} = 0.4198$$

This is the estimated variance of the error term $u$, used in constructing standard errors for each $\hat{\beta}_j$.

---

## Common Misconception

!!! warning "$R^2$ increasing when you add a variable means the variable belongs"
    **False.** $R^2$ mechanically increases with every added regressor, even noise. A variable with a $t$-statistic of 0.1 will still raise $R^2$. Always check (1) $\bar{R}^2$ — did it rise? (2) the $t$-statistic — is the coefficient statistically different from zero? (3) economic theory — does the variable belong in the model on substantive grounds? All three should support inclusion.

## Practice Prompts

1. **Algebraic property.** Prove that $\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i = 0$ in any OLS regression that includes an intercept. (*Hint:* The intercept column in $\mathbf{X}$ is a column of ones; use the orthogonality condition $\sum x_{i0}\hat{u}_i = 0$ where $x_{i0} = 1$.)

2. **Adjusted $R^2$ calculation.** A researcher estimates a model with $n = 50$, $k = 5$, and reports $R^2 = 0.72$. Calculate $\bar{R}^2$. Then suppose they add 5 more irrelevant regressors and $R^2$ rises to 0.76. Calculate the new $\bar{R}^2$ and explain whether the additional regressors improved the model.

3. **Model comparison.** Two models are estimated on the same $n=200$ sample:
   - Model A: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + u$; $\bar{R}^2 = 0.41$
   - Model B: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_3 + \beta_4 x_4 + u$; $\bar{R}^2 = 0.39$
   
   Which model do you prefer and why? What does it imply about $x_2, x_3, x_4$?
