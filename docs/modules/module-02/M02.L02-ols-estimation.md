## Explanation

### 1. The Regression Model

We postulate a **population linear regression model**:

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u$$

where:

- $y$ is the **dependent variable** (outcome we want to explain)
- $x$ is the **independent variable** (regressor, explanatory variable)
- $\beta_0$ is the **population intercept**
- $\beta_1$ is the **population slope** (the causal or associational effect of interest)
- $u$ is the **error term** (everything else that affects $y$, unobserved)

We observe $n$ data points $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$. The goal of **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)** is to find estimates $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ such that the fitted line:

$$\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i$$

is "as close as possible" to the observed data.

---

### 2. The OLS Objective: Minimise Squared Residuals

For any candidate line $(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1)$, define the **residual** for observation $i$ as:

$$\hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i$$

This is the vertical distance between the observed $y_i$ and the fitted value $\hat{y}_i$.

OLS chooses $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ to minimise the **sum of squared residuals (SSR)**:

$$\min_{\hat{\beta}_0,\, \hat{\beta}_1} \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i)^2$$

> **Why squared residuals?**  
> Squaring penalises large deviations heavily and treats positive/negative deviations symmetrically. Critically, squaring yields a smooth, convex objective function — this means the minimum is unique and can be found analytically by taking derivatives.

---

### 3. Derivation via First-Order Conditions

Let $Q(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i)^2$.

**Step 1 — Differentiate with respect to $\hat{\beta}_0$ and set to zero:**

$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial \hat{\beta}_0} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i) = 0$$

Dividing by $-2$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i) = 0 \tag{FOC1}$$

Expanding and dividing by $n$:

$$\bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} \tag{Intercept}$$

**Step 2 — Differentiate with respect to $\hat{\beta}_1$ and set to zero:**

$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial \hat{\beta}_1} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i) = 0$$

Dividing by $-2$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i) = 0 \tag{FOC2}$$

**Step 3 — Substitute the expression for $\hat{\beta}_0$ into FOC2.**

Substituting $\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \bigl(y_i - \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1(x_i - \bar{x})\bigr) = 0$$

Expanding:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \bar{y}) = \hat{\beta}_1 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i(x_i - \bar{x})$$

Using the identity $\sum x_i(x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2$ and $\sum x_i(y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = \hat{\beta}_1 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

Solving for $\hat{\beta}_1$ (provided $\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 \neq 0$, i.e. $x$ is not constant):

$$\boxed{\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}$$

And the intercept:

$$\boxed{\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}}$$

---

### 4. Algebraic Intuition: Slope as Scaled Covariance

The OLS slope can be written compactly in population-moment notation:

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(x, y)}{\widehat{\text{Var}}(x)}$$

where $\widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})$ and $\widehat{\text{Var}}(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})^2$ (the $n$-denominator versions cancel).

**Intuition:**

- If $x$ and $y$ move together (positive covariance), the slope is positive.
- The denominator $\text{Var}(x)$ *normalises* the slope — it answers: "how much does $y$ move for each unit of $x$, accounting for how spread out $x$ itself is?"
- If you multiply $x$ by 2, both numerator and denominator scale up, but denominator scales by 4 while numerator only by 2, so $\hat{\beta}_1$ halves. (We'll revisit this in M02.L04.)

---

### 5. Interpretation

Given the estimated equation $\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$:

- **$\hat{\beta}_1$**: A one-unit increase in $x$ is *associated with* a $\hat{\beta}_1$-unit change in $y$, on average (holding the rest of the model constant — though in simple regression there is nothing else to hold constant).
- **$\hat{\beta}_0$**: The predicted value of $y$ when $x = 0$. Often not economically meaningful; interpret cautiously if $x = 0$ is outside the data range.

> **Caution on "causal" language:** OLS gives the best linear *predictor* of $y$ given $x$. Whether $\hat{\beta}_1$ has a causal interpretation depends on additional assumptions (SLR.4: zero conditional mean error), not on the OLS algebra itself.

## Worked Example

### Dataset: Education and Hourly Wages

Suppose we observe $n = 5$ workers:

| $i$ | Years of Education ($x_i$) | Hourly Wage, \$ ($y_i$) |
|-----|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | 8 | 7.25 |
| 2 | 10 | 9.00 |
| 3 | 12 | 11.50 |
| 4 | 14 | 14.00 |
| 5 | 16 | 18.25 |

**Goal:** Estimate $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ for $\text{Wage}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Educ}_i + u_i$.

---

**Step 1: Compute means.**

$$\bar{x} = \frac{8 + 10 + 12 + 14 + 16}{5} = \frac{60}{5} = 12$$

$$\bar{y} = \frac{7.25 + 9.00 + 11.50 + 14.00 + 18.25}{5} = \frac{60.00}{5} = 12.00$$

---

**Step 2: Compute deviations and cross-products.**

| $i$ | $x_i - \bar{x}$ | $y_i - \bar{y}$ | $(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})$ | $(x_i-\bar{x})^2$ |
|-----|-----------------|-----------------|-------------------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | $8 - 12 = -4$ | $7.25 - 12 = -4.75$ | $(-4)(-4.75) = 19.00$ | 16 |
| 2 | $10 - 12 = -2$ | $9.00 - 12 = -3.00$ | $(-2)(-3.00) = 6.00$ | 4 |
| 3 | $12 - 12 = 0$ | $11.50 - 12 = -0.50$ | $(0)(-0.50) = 0.00$ | 0 |
| 4 | $14 - 12 = 2$ | $14.00 - 12 = 2.00$ | $(2)(2.00) = 4.00$ | 4 |
| 5 | $16 - 12 = 4$ | $18.25 - 12 = 6.25$ | $(4)(6.25) = 25.00$ | 16 |
| **Sum** | 0 | 0 | **54.00** | **40** |

*(Deviations always sum to zero — a useful check.)*

---

**Step 3: Calculate $\hat{\beta}_1$.**

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{54.00}{40} = 1.35$$

---

**Step 4: Calculate $\hat{\beta}_0$.**

$$\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} = 12.00 - 1.35 \times 12 = 12.00 - 16.20 = -4.20$$

---

**Step 5: Write the estimated equation.**

$$\widehat{\text{Wage}} = -4.20 + 1.35 \times \text{Educ}$$

---

**Step 6: Interpretation.**

- **Slope ($\hat{\beta}_1 = 1.35$):** Each additional year of education is associated with, on average, **\$1.35 more per hour** in wages.
- **Intercept ($\hat{\beta}_0 = -4.20$):** A worker with zero years of education is predicted to earn $-\$4.20$/hour — this is outside the data range (minimum $x = 8$) and is economically meaningless. Do not over-interpret.
- **Predicted value check:** For $x = 12$ (the mean): $\hat{y} = -4.20 + 1.35 \times 12 = -4.20 + 16.20 = 12.00 = \bar{y}$. ✓ The line passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.

---

## Common Misconception

> **"OLS minimises the sum of absolute residuals."**

This is false. OLS minimises $\sum \hat{u}_i^2$ — the **sum of *squared* residuals**. The method that minimises $\sum |\hat{u}_i|$ is called **Least Absolute Deviations (LAD)** regression, and it does not yield closed-form analytical formulas like OLS does.

Squaring matters for three reasons:

1. **Unique solution:** The squared objective is strictly convex, guaranteeing a unique minimum found by setting derivatives to zero. The absolute-value objective is piecewise linear and may have multiple solutions.
2. **Analytical tractability:** The closed-form formulas $\hat{\beta}_1 = \sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}) / \sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$ arise directly from the quadratic FOCs.
3. **Statistical theory:** The squared-residual criterion links naturally to the normal distribution and the Gauss-Markov theorem (covered in M02.L05).

The tradeoff is that squaring makes OLS **sensitive to outliers** (a single large $\hat{u}_i$ contributes $\hat{u}_i^2$ to the objective). LAD is more robust to outliers but lacks clean analytical properties.

## Practice Prompts

### Problem 1

A researcher collects data on 5 cities: population (in thousands) and average commute time (minutes):

| City | Population $x_i$ | Commute $y_i$ |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| A | 100 | 22 |
| B | 200 | 28 |
| C | 300 | 35 |
| D | 400 | 40 |
| E | 500 | 45 |

**(a)** Compute $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$.  
**(b)** Compute $\hat{\beta}_1$ and $\hat{\beta}_0$.  
**(c)** Interpret $\hat{\beta}_1$ in context.  
**(d)** What is the predicted commute time for a city of 350,000 people?

??? answer "Show Answer"
    **(a)**  $\bar{x} = (100+200+300+400+500)/5 = 300$; $\bar{y} = (22+28+35+40+45)/5 = 34$.

    **(b)**  

    | City | $x_i - 300$ | $y_i - 34$ | Product | $(x_i-300)^2$ |
    |------|------------|-----------|---------|--------------|
    | A | -200 | -12 | 2400 | 40000 |
    | B | -100 | -6 | 600 | 10000 |
    | C | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
    | D | 100 | 6 | 600 | 10000 |
    | E | 200 | 11 | 2200 | 40000 |
    | **Sum** | | | **5800** | **100000** |

    $\hat{\beta}_1 = 5800/100000 = 0.058$

    $\hat{\beta}_0 = 34 - 0.058 \times 300 = 34 - 17.4 = 16.6$

    **(c)** Each additional thousand residents in a city is associated with, on average, 0.058 additional minutes of commute time.

    **(d)** $\hat{y} = 16.6 + 0.058 \times 350 = 16.6 + 20.3 = 36.9$ minutes.

### Problem 2

You are given the following summary statistics for a sample of $n = 20$ students:

$$\bar{x} = 5.0 \text{ (hours studied)}, \quad \bar{y} = 70.0 \text{ (exam score)}$$
$$\sum_{i=1}^{20}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = 180, \quad \sum_{i=1}^{20}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 60$$

**(a)** Compute $\hat{\beta}_1$ and $\hat{\beta}_0$.  
**(b)** Predict the score for a student who studies 8 hours.  
**(c)** If the score variable is changed to a percentage of a 150-point scale (i.e. $y^* = y/150 \times 100$), how do $\hat{\beta}_0$, $\hat{\beta}_1$, and the fitted line change?

??? answer "Show Answer"
    **(a)** $\hat{\beta}_1 = 180/60 = 3.0$; $\hat{\beta}_0 = 70.0 - 3.0 \times 5.0 = 55.0$.  
    Estimated equation: $\widehat{\text{Score}} = 55.0 + 3.0 \times \text{Hours}$.

    **(b)** $\hat{y} = 55.0 + 3.0 \times 8 = 55.0 + 24.0 = 79.0$ points.

    **(c)** $y^* = (100/150) \cdot y$. Multiplying $y$ by the constant $c = 100/150 \approx 0.6\overline{6}$ scales both $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ by $c$:  
    $\hat{\beta}_0^* = 55.0 \times (100/150) \approx 36.67$; $\hat{\beta}_1^* = 3.0 \times (100/150) = 2.0$.  
    The fitted values simply rescale; $R^2$ is unchanged (covered in M02.L04).

### Problem 3

True or False — explain your reasoning for each:

**(a)** If $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0$, the OLS regression line is horizontal and $\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}$ for all $i$.  
**(b)** The OLS line always passes through the point $(0, 0)$.  
**(c)** You can have $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0$ even when $x$ and $y$ are strongly correlated.

??? answer "Show Answer"
    **(a)** **True.** If $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0$, then $\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - 0 \cdot \bar{x} = \bar{y}$, so $\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}$ for all $i$. The regression line is flat at the sample mean of $y$.

    **(b)** **False.** The OLS line passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, not $(0, 0)$ — unless it happens that $\bar{x} = 0$ and $\bar{y} = 0$. The intercept $\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}$ is specifically designed to enforce the $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ constraint.

    **(c)** **False.** $\hat{\beta}_1 = \widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y)/\widehat{\text{Var}}(x) = 0$ requires $\widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y) = 0$, which means $x$ and $y$ are sample-uncorrelated. If $x$ and $y$ are strongly correlated, $\widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y) \neq 0$, so $\hat{\beta}_1 \neq 0$.
