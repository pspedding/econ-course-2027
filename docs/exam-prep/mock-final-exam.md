**Question 1**

*Section A · Price Controls*


A price ceiling set below the equilibrium price will usually create:

- A) A surplus
- B) A shortage
- C) No effect on the market
- D) Higher producer surplus

??? success "Show Answer"
    **Answer: B) A shortage**

    A binding price ceiling prevents the price from rising to the equilibrium level, so quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.

**Question 3**

*Section A · Taxes and Incidence*


A tax of $20 per unit is imposed on consumers. Demand is P = 60 − 4Q and supply is P = 6Q. How much more do consumers pay, and how much less do sellers receive?

- A) Consumers pay $12 more; sellers receive $8 less
- B) Consumers pay $8 more; sellers receive $12 less
- C) Consumers pay $20 more; sellers receive $0 less
- D) Consumers pay $4 more; sellers receive $16 less

??? success "Show Answer"
    **Answer: B) Consumers pay $8 more; sellers receive $12 less**

    Pre-tax equilibrium:
    60 − 4Q = 6Q
    60 = 10Q
    Q* = 6
    P* = 36
    
    With a $20 tax on consumers:
    Pc = Ps + 20
    Demand: Pc = 60 − 4Q
    Supply: Ps = 6Q
    
    So:
    60 − 4Q = 6Q + 20
    40 = 10Q
    Q = 4
    
    Ps = 6(4) = 24 and Pc = 24 + 20 = 44.
    
    Consumers pay 44 − 36 = **$8 more**.
    Sellers receive 24 − 36 = **$12 less**.

**Question 4**

*Section A · Tax Incidence*


If supply is perfectly elastic and a tax is imposed on sellers, then:

- A) Sellers bear the full tax burden
- B) Buyers bear the full tax burden
- C) The burden is split equally
- D) No deadweight loss is created

??? success "Show Answer"
    **Answer: B) Buyers bear the full tax burden**

    A perfectly elastic supply curve means sellers will supply any amount at a given net price. A tax on sellers shifts the supply curve upward by the tax amount, so buyers pay the full increase in price. Because quantity falls, there is still deadweight loss.

**Question 5**

*Section A · Taxes and Welfare*


Petrol demand is D = 280 − 4Q and supply is S = 10 + Q. A tax of $20 per unit is imposed on consumers. What are the post-tax quantity, government revenue, and deadweight loss?

- A) Q = 54, revenue = $1080, DWL = $0
- B) Q = 50, revenue = $1000, DWL = $40
- C) Q = 50, revenue = $1080, DWL = $100
- D) Q = 54, revenue = $1000, DWL = $40

??? success "Show Answer"
    **Answer: B) Q = 50, revenue = $1000, DWL = $40**

    Pre-tax equilibrium:
    280 − 4Q = 10 + Q
    270 = 5Q
    **Q* = 54**, **P* = 64**
    
    With a consumer tax:
    Pc = Ps + 20
    Demand: Pc = 280 − 4Q
    Supply: Ps = 10 + Q
    
    So:
    280 − 4Q = 10 + Q + 20
    250 = 5Q
    **Q = 50**
    
    Revenue = 20 × 50 = **$1000**
    
    DWL = 0.5 × 4 × 20 = **$40**

**Question 21**  
*Negative externalities and policy*  
20 marks

(i) Draw and explain a market with a negative externality, showing MPC, MSC, the market equilibrium, the socially efficient quantity, and deadweight loss.  
(ii) Using the lead battery numbers, calculate the Pigouvian tax: demand P = 200 − 2Q, MPC = 20 + Q, MEC = Q.  
(iii) Why might Coase bargaining fail in environmental pollution cases?

??? success "Show Answer"
    **(i) Diagram and explanation**
    
    A negative production externality means the private market ignores part of the cost imposed on third parties.
    
    - **MPC** is the firm's private supply curve.
    - **MSC = MPC + MEC** lies above MPC.
    - The market equilibrium occurs where demand intersects MPC, so output is too high.
    - The socially efficient outcome is where demand intersects MSC, so output is lower.
    - The deadweight loss is the triangle between demand, MSC, and the market quantity between Q* and Q_opt.
    
    In words: the market overproduces because it only considers private cost, not social cost.
    
    **(ii) Pigouvian tax**
    
    Competitive equilibrium:
    200 − 2Q = 20 + Q
    180 = 3Q
    **Q* = 60**
    
    Price:
    P* = 20 + 60 = **80**
    
    Social marginal cost:
    MSC = MPC + MEC = (20 + Q) + Q = **20 + 2Q**
    
    Efficient output:
    200 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q
    180 = 4Q
    **Q_opt = 45**
    
    Pigouvian tax at the efficient quantity:
    MEC = 45
    So the tax should be **$45 per unit**.
    
    **(iii) Why Coase may fail**
    
    Coase bargaining often fails because environmental harm usually affects many people, so transaction costs are high. It is hard to identify every affected party, measure damages precisely, and prevent free-riding. Information is also imperfect, and bargaining across many parties is costly. For pollution, these frictions make market bargaining much harder than the Coase theorem’s idealised assumptions.

**Question 22**  
*Public goods and common resources*  
20 marks

(i) Explain the free-rider problem with an example.  
(ii) For the lighthouse example, Alex has MBA = 24 − G and Beatrice has MBB = 12 − 0.5G, with MC = 30. Find the efficient quantity of G.  
(iii) How does the tragedy of the commons differ from the public goods problem?

??? success "Show Answer"
    **(i) Free-rider problem**
    
    A free rider is someone who enjoys the benefit of a public good without paying for it. Because public goods are non-excludable, individuals know they cannot easily be excluded, so they may withhold payment and hope others fund the good.
    
    Example: If a town finances a fireworks display, each resident may prefer others to pay while still enjoying the show. If everyone reasons this way, the private market underprovides the good.
    
    **(ii) Lighthouse efficiency**
    
    Vertical summation:
    MSB = (24 − G) + (12 − 0.5G)
    = **36 − 1.5G**
    
    Efficient quantity:
    36 − 1.5G = 30
    1.5G = 6
    **G = 4**
    
    So the efficient number of lighthouses is **4**.
    
    **(iii) Commons vs public goods**
    
    The **public goods problem** is caused by **non-excludability** and **non-rivalry**, which leads to under-provision because of free-riding.
    
    The **tragedy of the commons** occurs with **rival but non-excludable** goods. The problem there is overuse, because each person’s use reduces availability for others but no one can be easily excluded.
    
    So public goods are typically **under-provided**, while common resources are typically **over-exploited**.

**Question 23**  
*Price floors and trade*  
20 marks

(i) In a labour market, demand is D = 400 − 2w and supply is S = 240 + 2w. Find the equilibrium wage and employment. Then suppose a price floor of $50 is imposed. Find employment, unemployment, and deadweight loss.  
(ii) Explain comparative advantage.  
(iii) Why do economists generally favour free trade even when it creates winners and losers domestically?

??? success "Show Answer"
    **(i) Labour market calculation**
    
    Equilibrium:
    400 − 2w = 240 + 2w
    160 = 4w
    **w* = 40**
    
    Employment at equilibrium:
    Q* = 400 − 2(40) = **320**
    
    With a price floor of $50:
    Quantity demanded:
    Q_D = 400 − 2(50) = **300**
    
    Quantity supplied:
    Q_S = 240 + 2(50) = **340**
    
    Employment is limited by demand, so actual employment = **300**.
    
    Unemployment = Q_S − Q_D = 340 − 300 = **40**
    
    Deadweight loss:
    DWL = 0.5 × (50 − 40) × (340 − 300)
    = 0.5 × 10 × 40
    = **$200**
    
    **(ii) Comparative advantage**
    
    Comparative advantage means producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer or country. It is about relative efficiency, not absolute productivity.
    
    **(iii) Why economists favour free trade**
    
    Economists generally favour free trade because it allows countries to specialise according to comparative advantage, raising total world output and welfare. Even if some domestic workers or firms lose, the total gains from trade are large enough that the winners can in principle compensate the losers and still be better off. Free trade also increases variety, lowers prices, and encourages more efficient resource allocation.
