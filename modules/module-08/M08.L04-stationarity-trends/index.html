    Lesson M08.L04: Stationarity, Trends, and Unit Root Tests

    Field Detail
    Module 08 — Time Series Basics
    Level Graduate (ECMT5001 S1 2027)
    Duration 30–35 min
    Learning Objective Define covariance stationarity; distinguish deterministic from stochastic trends; understand the ADF unit root test; know when to detrend versus first-difference.
    Provenance Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, Ch 10.4–10.5

    Explanation

    1. Stationarity

    A time series \(\{y_t\}\) is covariance stationary (weakly stationary) if its first two moments are time-invariant:

    \[\begin{aligned} E(y_t) &= \mu \quad &&\text{(constant mean)} \\ \text{Var}(y_t) &= \sigma^2 < \infty \quad &&\text{(constant, finite variance)} \\ \text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-h}) &= \gamma(h) \quad &&\text{(depends only on lag } h\text{, not on } t\text{)} \end{aligned}\]

    Covariance stationarity is also called weak stationarity (as opposed to strict stationarity, which requires the entire joint distribution to be time-invariant).

    Why does stationarity matter? - Standard asymptotic theory (LLN, CLT) for time series requires stationarity or near-stationarity. - Non-stationary series have moments that change over time, making the past a poor guide to the future and standard inference invalid. - Spurious regressions (M08.L03) arise from regressing non-stationary series on each other.

    Examples: - Stationary: stock returns, GDP growth rate (%), CPI inflation (if in a stable policy regime). - Non-stationary: GDP level, stock prices, nominal wages — all trend upward over time.

    2. Deterministic Trend (Trend-Stationary Process)

    A deterministic trend model:

    \[y_t = \alpha + \beta t + u_t, \quad u_t \sim \text{stationary}\]

    Here the mean changes linearly over time (\(E(y_t) = \alpha + \beta t\)), but the variance is constant (\(\text{Var}(y_t) = \sigma_u^2\)). The series "wanders" above and below the trend line but always reverts to it.

    This series is NOT stationary (non-constant mean), but it is trend-stationary: once you remove the linear time trend, the residuals \(u_t\) are stationary.

    Solution: include \(t\) as a regressor (detrending):

    1. Regress \(y_t\) on a constant and \(t\): \(y_t = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} t + \hat{u}_t\).
    2. Use the detrended residuals \(\hat{u}_t\) (or the detrended \(y\)) in subsequent regressions.

    Alternatively, simply include \(t\) as a control variable in the regression of interest. This soaks up the common trend and avoids spurious correlation between \(y_t\) and other trending series.

    3. Stochastic Trend / Unit Root

    A random walk (the canonical stochastic trend model):

    \[y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t, \quad e_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, \sigma_e^2)\]

    Starting from \(y_0\):

    \[y_t = y_0 + \sum_{s=1}^t e_s\]

    Key properties: $\(E(y_t) = y_0 \quad \text{(constant mean)}\)$ $\(\text{Var}(y_t) = t \sigma_e^2 \quad \text{(grows without bound)}\)$

    The variance increases linearly with \(t\) — this series has a stochastic trend: it wanders further and further from its starting point over time, with no tendency to revert. The shocks are permanent: a shock \(e_t\) shifts the level of \(y\) permanently.

    Why "unit root"? Express the random walk as:

    \[y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + e_t\]

    with \(\rho = 1\). In the AR(1) representation, the autoregressive coefficient equals 1 (the "unit root"). For \(|\rho| < 1\), the process is stationary (mean-reverting). For \(\rho = 1\), the process is non-stationary.

    Random walk with drift:

    \[y_t = \mu + y_{t-1} + e_t\]

    Now \(E(y_t) = y_0 + \mu t\) — trending upward (or downward) on average, with a variance still growing as \(t\sigma_e^2\). Most economic levels (GDP, price indices) follow a random walk with positive drift.

    4. Contrast: Deterministic vs Stochastic Trend

    Feature Deterministic Trend Stochastic Trend (Unit Root)
    Mean \(\alpha + \beta t\) (non-constant) \(y_0\) (random walk) or \(y_0 + \mu t\) (drift)
    Variance Constant \(\sigma_u^2\) Grows: \(t \sigma_e^2\)
    Shock persistence Temporary (shocks decay) Permanent (shocks accumulate)
    Remedy Detrend (subtract \(\hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} t\)) First-difference (\(\Delta y_t\))
    After remedy Stationary Stationary (if one unit root)

    Critical point: If a series has a unit root, detrending does not make it stationary — you must first-difference. Applying the wrong remedy leaves you with a non-stationary series and invalid inference.

    5. Integrated Processes

    A process \(y_t\) that becomes stationary after \(d\) first-differences is said to be integrated of order \(d\), written \(y_t \sim I(d)\):

    • \(I(0)\): already stationary.
    • \(I(1)\): one unit root; \(\Delta y_t \sim I(0)\).
    • \(I(2)\): two unit roots; \(\Delta^2 y_t \sim I(0)\) (rare in economics).

    Most macroeconomic levels (GDP, prices, nominal wages) are \(I(1)\). Their growth rates (log differences) are \(I(0)\).

    6. Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test

    The ADF test is the standard formal test for a unit root.

    Null hypothesis: \(H_0\): \(y_t\) has a unit root (is non-stationary, \(I(1)\)). Alternative: \(H_1\): \(y_t\) is stationary (\(I(0)\)).

    Regression form:

    \[\Delta y_t = \alpha + \theta y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^p \gamma_j \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\]

    The augmentation terms \(\sum_{j=1}^p \gamma_j \Delta y_{t-j}\) absorb any serial correlation in the errors (the Dickey-Fuller test without augmentation assumes i.i.d. errors; the ADF is more general).

    Test statistic: \(t_\theta = \hat{\theta} / SE(\hat{\theta})\)

    Decision rule: - Reject \(H_0\) (conclude stationary) if \(t_\theta < \text{ADF critical value}\) (e.g. \(-2.89\) at 5% for no trend; \(-3.45\) at 5% with trend). - The ADF critical values are not standard normal — they are more negative (require a larger magnitude to reject) because the distribution shifts under the unit-root null. - Fail to reject \(H_0\) → conclude \(I(1)\); take first differences.

    Lag length selection for \(p\): Use AIC/BIC or start with a long lag and drop insignificant ones.

    Specification choices: - No constant, no trend (rarely appropriate in economics). - With constant (most common for stationary-around-mean series). - With constant and linear trend (for potentially trend-stationary series).

    7. Solution: First Differencing

    If a series is \(I(1)\), first-differencing produces a stationary \(I(0)\) series:

    \[\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\]

    Examples: - \(\ln(\text{GDP}_t)\) is \(I(1)\) → \(\Delta \ln(\text{GDP}_t) \approx g_t\) (GDP growth rate) is \(I(0)\). - \(\ln(\text{CPI}_t)\) is \(I(1)\) → \(\Delta \ln(\text{CPI}_t) \approx \pi_t\) (inflation rate) is \(I(0)\). - Nominal interest rate \(r_t\) is often \(I(1)\) → \(\Delta r_t\) is \(I(0)\).

    Trade-off of differencing: You lose information about long-run levels. If you suspect a long-run cointegrating relationship between two \(I(1)\) series, first-differencing discards that relationship — you should instead use a vector error correction model (VECM) (advanced topic).

    Worked Example

    Is Australian GDP stationary? Levels vs. growth rates.

    Setup. Consider quarterly Australian real GDP (\(Y_t\)) and the quarterly GDP growth rate (\(g_t = 100 \times \Delta \ln Y_t\)), 1960Q1–2023Q4 (\(T = 256\)).

    Step 1. Visual inspection.

    • \(Y_t\) (level): trends steadily upward from ~$100bn to ~$600bn (2015 AUD). Clearly non-constant mean.
    • \(g_t\) (growth rate): fluctuates around approximately 0.8% per quarter with no obvious trend. Plausible stationarity.

    Step 2. ADF test — GDP level (\(Y_t\)).

    Specification: with constant and linear trend (because \(Y_t\) trends upward).

    \[\Delta Y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \theta Y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^4 \gamma_j \Delta Y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\]

    Suppose \(t_\theta = -1.85\). ADF 5% critical value (with trend) = \(-3.45\).

    Since \(-1.85 > -3.45\): fail to reject \(H_0\). Conclude \(Y_t\) has a unit root — it is \(I(1)\).

    Step 3. ADF test — GDP growth rate (\(g_t\)).

    Specification: with constant only (growth rate fluctuates around a positive mean).

    \[\Delta g_t = \alpha + \theta g_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^4 \gamma_j \Delta g_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\]

    Suppose \(t_\theta = -5.42\). ADF 5% critical value (with constant) = \(-2.89\).

    Since \(-5.42 < -2.89\): reject \(H_0\). Conclude \(g_t\) is stationary — it is \(I(0)\).

    Step 4. Conclusion and implication.

    Series ADF stat 5% CV Decision Order
    \(Y_t\) (level) −1.85 −3.45 Fail to reject \(H_0\) \(I(1)\)
    \(g_t\) (growth) −5.42 −2.89 Reject \(H_0\) \(I(0)\)

    Implication: Regressions using GDP levels are at risk of spurious regression unless the other variable is also \(I(1)\) and the two series are cointegrated. Regressions using GDP growth rates are valid under standard OLS asymptotics (given stationarity).

    Common Misconception

    "Detrending (removing a linear time trend) removes all non-stationarity."

    This is false. Detrending (subtracting \(\hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} t\)) only works if the series is trend-stationary — i.e. non-stationary only because of a deterministic trend, with stationary errors.

    If the series has a stochastic trend (unit root), detrending does not produce a stationary series. The detrended series will still have a variance that grows over time (because the unit-root shocks accumulate even around a linear trend). You must first-difference, not detrend.

    How to tell which applies: Use the ADF test. Include a time trend in the ADF regression. If you reject the unit-root null, the series is trend-stationary (detrend). If you fail to reject, it has a unit root (first-difference).

    Practical cost of the wrong remedy: - First-differencing a trend-stationary series: over-differencing → introduces unnecessary moving-average structure in errors. - Detrending a unit-root series: fails to achieve stationarity → spurious regression, invalid inference.

    Practice Prompts

    1. Stationarity check. Consider three time series:
    2. \(A_t\): Australian CPI level (1990–2023, monthly).
    3. \(B_t\): Australian CPI inflation rate (monthly change in log CPI).
    4. \(C_t\): 10-year government bond yield (%).

    (a) For each series, state whether you would expect it to be stationary or non-stationary, and why. (b) For any non-stationary series, suggest the appropriate transformation to induce stationarity. (c) What specification (no trend, constant only, constant + trend) would you use in the ADF test for each series? Justify.

    1. ADF test interpretation. A researcher tests whether the monthly Australian unemployment rate (\(U_t\)) is stationary using an ADF test with constant and no trend. She finds \(t_\theta = -2.31\) and the 5% ADF critical value is \(-2.89\). (a) State the null and alternative hypotheses of the ADF test. (b) What is the conclusion at the 5% level? (c) What should she do before running a regression of \(U_t\) on other economic variables? Give two specific steps.

    2. Random walk properties. A random walk is defined by \(y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t\), \(e_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, 4)\), \(y_0 = 0\). (a) Compute \(\text{Var}(y_{25})\). (b) Explain why this series is not covariance stationary. (c) Show that \(\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\) is stationary. What are its mean and variance? (d) Explain in words what it means for shocks to be "permanent" in a random walk, vs "transitory" in a stationary AR(1).
