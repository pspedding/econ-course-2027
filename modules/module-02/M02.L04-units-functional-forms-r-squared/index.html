    Lesson M02.L04: Units, Functional Forms, and R-Squared

    Field Detail
    Module M02 — Simple Linear Regression (Wooldridge Chapter 2)
    Level Graduate
    Duration 35 minutes
    Learning Objective Interpret \(R^2\) correctly; predict how coefficient estimates change under rescaling; choose and interpret log-linear and log-log functional forms.
    Provenance Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 7th ed., Chapter 2, Sections 2.4 (units and functional form) and 2.3 (\(R^2\))

    Explanation

    1. R-Squared: Definition and Interpretation

    From M02.L03, we established the decomposition \(SST = SSE + SSR\). This motivates:

    \[R^2 = \frac{SSE}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST}\]

    Interpretation: \(R^2\) is the fraction of total sample variation in \(y\) that is explained (linearly) by \(x\).

    Key properties:

    Fact Explanation
    \(R^2 \in [0, 1]\) SSE and SSR are non-negative and sum to SST
    \(R^2 = 0\) \(x\) explains none of the variation in \(y\); \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0\) and \(\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}\) for all \(i\)
    \(R^2 = 1\) Perfect fit: every observation lies on the regression line; \(\hat{u}_i = 0\) for all \(i\)
    \(R^2 = r_{xy}^2\) In simple regression, \(R^2\) equals the square of the sample correlation between \(x\) and \(y\)

    2. Effect of Changing Units on OLS Estimates

    A critical skill: knowing how \(\hat{\beta}_0\), \(\hat{\beta}_1\), and \(R^2\) respond when you rescale variables.

    Case A: Multiply the dependent variable \(y\) by a constant \(c\)

    Define \(y^* = c \cdot y\). Running OLS of \(y^*\) on \(x\):

    \[\hat{\beta}_1^* = c \cdot \hat{\beta}_1, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0^* = c \cdot \hat{\beta}_0, \qquad R^2 \text{ unchanged}\]

    Intuition: Every \(y_i\) is scaled by \(c\), so every \(\hat{y}_i\) must also scale by \(c\) to minimise the new SSR. Both SSE and SST scale by \(c^2\), so their ratio \(R^2\) is unchanged.

    Example: If wages are in dollars and you convert to cents (\(c = 100\)), the slope multiplies by 100 (dollars-per-year becomes cents-per-year), but \(R^2\) stays the same.

    Case B: Multiply the independent variable \(x\) by a constant \(c\)

    Define \(x^* = c \cdot x\). Running OLS of \(y\) on \(x^*\):

    \[\hat{\beta}_1^* = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1}{c}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0^* = \hat{\beta}_0, \qquad R^2 \text{ unchanged}\]

    Intuition: A one-unit change in \(x^*\) corresponds to a \(c\)-unit change in the original \(x\). So the slope (effect per unit of regressor) must be divided by \(c\) to keep predicted values identical. The intercept (value when \(x^* = 0\), i.e. \(x = 0\)) is unchanged.

    Example: If education is measured in months instead of years (\(c = 12\)), the slope divides by 12, but \(R^2\) and \(\hat{\beta}_0\) are unchanged.

    Summary table:

    Change \(\hat{\beta}_1\) \(\hat{\beta}_0\) \(R^2\)
    \(y \to c \cdot y\) \(\times c\) \(\times c\) unchanged
    \(x \to c \cdot x\) \(\div c\) unchanged unchanged
    \(y \to c \cdot y\) and \(x \to d \cdot x\) \(\times (c/d)\) \(\times c\) unchanged

    3. Functional Forms: Why Log Transformations?

    The linear model \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u\) assumes a constant additive effect: one unit of \(x\) always changes \(y\) by \(\beta_1\), regardless of the level of \(x\). For many economic variables, this is unrealistic.

    Common reasons to use \(\log(y)\) or \(\log(x)\):

    1. Right-skewed distributions: Wages, income, prices, and GDP are typically right-skewed with a few very large values. \(\log(\text{wage})\) is much more symmetric and closer to normally distributed.
    2. Elasticity interpretation: Taking logs converts the slope into a percentage-change interpretation — often more natural in economics.
    3. Heteroskedasticity reduction: Log transforms often stabilise variance across the range of \(x\) (relevant for inference; covered in later modules).

    Note: \(\log\) in econometrics almost always means the natural logarithm (base \(e\), denoted \(\ln\) in mathematics). Wooldridge writes \(\log\) throughout and means \(\ln\).

    4. The Log-Linear Model (Semi-Elasticity)

    Model:

    \[\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u\]

    Interpretation of \(\hat{\beta}_1\):

    Using the approximation \(\Delta \log(y) \approx \Delta y / y\) (valid for small changes):

    \[\hat{\beta}_1 \approx \frac{\% \Delta y}{100 \cdot \Delta x}\]

    More precisely: a one-unit increase in \(x\) is associated with approximately a \(100 \cdot \hat{\beta}_1\)% change in \(y\).

    This is called a semi-elasticity — the dependent variable is in logs but the independent variable is in levels.

    Example: If \(\log(\text{wage}) = 1.2 + 0.08 \times \text{Educ} + u\):
    → One additional year of education is associated with approximately an 8% increase in wages.
    → More precisely: the multiplicative factor is \(e^{0.08} \approx 1.0833\), so wages increase by about 8.33%.

    5. The Log-Log Model (Constant Elasticity)

    Model:

    \[\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(x) + u\]

    Interpretation of \(\hat{\beta}_1\):

    \[\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\% \Delta y}{\% \Delta x} \quad \text{(elasticity)}\]

    A 1% increase in \(x\) is associated with a \(\hat{\beta}_1\)% change in \(y\). The slope is a constant elasticity.

    Example: If \(\log(\text{sales}) = 2.0 - 0.5 \times \log(\text{price}) + u\):
    → A 1% increase in price is associated with a 0.5% decrease in sales.
    → \(\hat{\beta}_1 = -0.5\) is the price elasticity of demand (inelastic demand).

    6. When to Use Each Form

    Model \(x\) \(y\) Slope Interpretation
    Level-Level levels levels \(\Delta y\) per 1-unit \(\Delta x\)
    Log-Level (semi-elasticity) levels logs \(\approx \%\Delta y\) per 1-unit \(\Delta x\)
    Level-Log logs levels \(\Delta y\) per 1% \(\Delta x\)
    Log-Log (elasticity) logs logs \(\%\Delta y\) per 1% \(\Delta x\)

    Rule of thumb: Take the log of a variable if: - It has a strongly right-skewed distribution (positive values only, large range) - You want to interpret the coefficient as a percentage or elasticity - The relationship between \(y\) and \(x\) appears curved (concave) rather than linear

    7. A Note on R-Squared Across Models

    You cannot compare \(R^2\) across models with different dependent variables.

    A log-wage regression (\(\log(\text{wage})\) as \(y\)) and a wage-level regression (\(\text{wage}\) as \(y\)) have different SSTs and are not comparable by \(R^2\).

    Is a low R² bad? Not necessarily. \(R^2\) measures how much variation in \(y\) is explained by a single regressor. In cross-sectional economic data:

    • A regression of wages on education alone with \(R^2 = 0.15\) is typical and informative
    • The low \(R^2\) reflects that wages depend on many factors (ability, experience, employer, location) — not that the education coefficient is wrong or unimportant
    • If \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is statistically significant (tested in Module 4) and economically meaningful in magnitude, the regression adds real value regardless of \(R^2\)

    Key principle: \(R^2\) measures fit, not the validity, significance, or economic importance of a regression coefficient. Researchers who chase high \(R^2\) by adding irrelevant variables are making a mistake (the adjusted-\(R^2\) problem is addressed in later modules on multiple regression).

    Worked Example

    Setting: Log-Wage Regression

    A researcher estimates the following log-linear model on \(n = 526\) workers from the CPS wage dataset (from Wooldridge, Chapter 2):

    \[\widehat{\log(\text{wage})} = 0.584 + 0.083 \times \text{Educ}\]

    with \(R^2 = 0.186\).

    Interpretation of the slope:

    \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.083\): Each additional year of education is associated with approximately a 8.3% increase in hourly wage.

    (More precisely: \(e^{0.083} - 1 = 0.0865\), so about 8.65% — the approximation \(\approx 8.3\%\) is good for small values.)

    Interpretation of the intercept:

    \(\hat{\beta}_0 = 0.584\): A worker with zero years of education is predicted to have \(\log(\text{wage}) = 0.584\), corresponding to \(\text{wage} = e^{0.584} \approx \$1.79\)/hour. Again, this is extrapolation far outside the data range (minimum education in the sample is typically 0–1 years) and should not be taken literally.

    Predicted wage for a college graduate (16 years of education):

    \[\widehat{\log(\text{wage})} = 0.584 + 0.083 \times 16 = 0.584 + 1.328 = 1.912\]
    \[\widehat{\text{wage}} = e^{1.912} \approx \$6.77 \text{ per hour}\]

    (Note: this dataset is from the early 1990s — the wage level reflects that era.)

    Interpretation of \(R^2 = 0.186\):

    Education alone explains approximately 18.6% of the variation in log wages. The remaining 81.4% reflects other factors (experience, industry, location, ability, etc.). This is a typical result for a single-variable wage regression and does not indicate a flawed model.

    Effect of a units change: Suppose wages are measured in cents rather than dollars (\(y^* = 100 \times \text{wage}\)). Since we're working with \(\log(y^*)\):

    \[\log(y^*) = \log(100 \times \text{wage}) = \log(100) + \log(\text{wage}) = 4.605 + \log(\text{wage})\]

    So \(\hat{\beta}_0^* = 0.584 + 4.605 = 5.189\), while \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.083\) is unchanged (the log transform absorbs the scale change into the intercept), and \(R^2\) is unchanged.

    Common Misconception

    "A low R² means the regression is useless."

    This is one of the most persistent misconceptions in applied econometrics.

    \(R^2\) measures goodness of fit — how much of the variation in \(y\) the model explains. But the research question is often not "how much of \(y\) do I explain?" but rather "what is the effect of \(x\) on \(y\)?"

    Consider this thought experiment: Suppose we regress individual annual income on whether the person was born in January vs. all other months. The \(R^2\) will be tiny — month of birth explains almost none of income variation. But in a randomised experiment, even a tiny \(R^2\) provides an unbiased estimate of the treatment effect; the regression is perfectly valid.

    The relevant criteria for a useful regression are:

    1. Is \(\hat{\beta}_1\) statistically significantly different from zero? (Hypothesis testing, Module 4)
    2. Is the magnitude of \(\hat{\beta}_1\) economically meaningful?
    3. Are the assumptions (especially SLR.4: \(E(u|x) = 0\)) plausibly satisfied?

    \(R^2\) answers none of these questions. Maximising \(R^2\) by adding variables indiscriminately can actually harm inference (see adjusted-\(R^2\) and overfitting in Module 3).

    Practice Prompts

    Problem 1

    A regression of housing price (in $'000s) on house size (in square feet) yields:

    \[\widehat{\text{Price}} = 40.0 + 0.12 \times \text{Size}, \qquad R^2 = 0.62\]

    (a) Interpret \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.12\) in context.
    (b) If prices are instead measured in dollars (not $'000s), what is the new \(\hat{\beta}_1^*\)?
    (c) If size is instead measured in hundreds of square feet, what is the new \(\hat{\beta}_1^*\)?
    (d) In both (b) and (c), what happens to \(R^2\)?

    Show Answer

    (a) Each additional square foot of house size is associated with, on average, $120 more in house price (since price is in $'000s, so 0.12 × $1000 = $120 per sq ft).

    (b) Converting price to dollars multiplies \(y\) by 1000 (\(c = 1000\)):
    \(\hat{\beta}_1^* = 0.12 \times 1000 = 120\) (dollars per square foot).
    \(\hat{\beta}_0^* = 40.0 \times 1000 = 40{,}000\) dollars.

    (c) Converting size to hundreds of sq ft multiplies \(x\) by \(c = 100\):
    \(\hat{\beta}_1^* = 0.12 / 100 \times 100 = 0.12 \times 100 / 100\)... more carefully: if \(x^* = x/100\) (size in hundreds), we're dividing \(x\) by 100, i.e. \(c = 1/100\), so \(\hat{\beta}_1^* = 0.12 \div (1/100) = 12\).
    Interpretation: each additional 100 sq ft is associated with $12,000 more in price (since price is still in $'000s: \(12 \times \$1000 = \$12{,}000\)). \(\hat{\beta}_0\) unchanged at 40.0.

    (d) \(R^2 = 0.62\) in all cases. \(R^2\) is invariant to linear rescaling of \(x\) or \(y\).

    Problem 2

    A researcher estimates two models on wages:

    • Model A (level-level): \(\widehat{\text{Wage}} = -4.20 + 1.35 \times \text{Educ}\), \(R^2 = 0.974\)
    • Model B (log-level): \(\widehat{\log(\text{Wage})} = 1.05 + 0.09 \times \text{Educ}\), \(R^2 = 0.961\)

    (a) Interpret \(\hat{\beta}_1\) in Model A and Model B.
    (b) Can you conclude Model A is better than Model B because its \(R^2\) is higher?
    (c) What predicted wage does each model give for \(\text{Educ} = 14\)?

    Show Answer

    (a) Model A: Each additional year of education is associated with $1.35/hour more in wages (level effect). Model B: Each additional year of education is associated with approximately 9% more in wages (semi-elasticity). Model B is usually preferred for wages because it respects the lower bound of zero (log-wage can be any real number; predicted wage is always positive) and captures the multiplicative nature of wage growth.

    (b) No. The \(R^2\) values come from regressions with different dependent variables (Wage vs. \(\log(\text{Wage})\)). SST for wages (\(\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2\)) is completely different from SST for log-wages. The \(R^2\) values are not comparable. Model selection between these functional forms should be based on economic theory, distributional assumptions, and specification tests — not \(R^2\) comparison.

    (c) Model A: \(\widehat{\text{Wage}} = -4.20 + 1.35 \times 14 = -4.20 + 18.90 = \$14.70\)/hr.
    Model B: \(\widehat{\log(\text{Wage})} = 1.05 + 0.09 \times 14 = 1.05 + 1.26 = 2.31\); \(\widehat{\text{Wage}} = e^{2.31} \approx \$10.07\)/hr.

    Problem 3

    A log-log model is estimated for coffee sales:

    \[\widehat{\log(\text{Cups})} = 8.5 - 1.2 \times \log(\text{Price})\]

    (a) Interpret \(\hat{\beta}_1 = -1.2\).
    (b) Is demand for coffee elastic or inelastic with respect to price?
    (c) If price rises by 10%, what is the predicted percentage change in cups sold?
    (d) A competitor's model uses levels: \(\widehat{\text{Cups}} = 5000 - 800 \times \text{Price}\). Can you compare the two models' \(R^2\) values? Why or why not?

    Show Answer

    (a) \(\hat{\beta}_1 = -1.2\) is the price elasticity of demand: a 1% increase in price is associated with a 1.2% decrease in cups sold.

    (b) Since \(|\hat{\beta}_1| = 1.2 > 1\), demand is elastic with respect to price — consumers are relatively price-sensitive for coffee in this sample.

    (c) A 10% price increase → predicted change in cups = \(-1.2 \times 10\% = -12\%\).

    (d) No. The dependent variable in the log-log model is \(\log(\text{Cups})\) while the level model uses \(\text{Cups}\). These have different SSTs and the \(R^2\) values are not comparable. To compare models with different functional forms for \(y\), researchers typically use information criteria (AIC, BIC) or correlation-based measures that account for the transformation.
